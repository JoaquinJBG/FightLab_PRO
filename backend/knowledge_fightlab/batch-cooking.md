# Batch cooking

> Part of FightLab's evidence annex. Food-safety and organisation rules for cooking a week's meals in one session (batch cooking), as used by the menus, the batch-session screen, the eve-of-day reminders and the shopping list. Rules, not a literature review. Source IDs refer to sources.md in this folder. This content belongs to FightLab and is licensed CC BY 4.0; it is not part of ai-personal-coach. It was researched with the method of ai-personal-coach's docs/research-methodology.md: every source was opened (a PDF's text extracted from the downloaded file), and anything that could not be opened, or did not say what was claimed, was dropped (open it or drop it); see dropped-claims.md. Evidence checked on 2026-11-09.

## How to read this file

Grades are used as in combat-sports.md. Here **strong** means that several food-safety agencies give the same guidance; **moderate** means agency guidance that differs between agencies, or a single agency or extension service; **emerging** means only cooking guides or blogs, presented as reasonable practice. "FightLab convention" marks a product choice (a number, a split of days, a word list) that the app needs and no source states; it is never presented as evidence. The sources are mostly national food-safety agencies (Spain's AESAN, the UK's FSA, Ireland's FSAI, the USDA), and they do not always agree: the rules follow the most cautious one and say where the others differ.

## Key rules at a glance

- Start the longest cooking first and run the oven and the hob in parallel; the app's per-appliance timeline and its substitutions are FightLab conventions. (emerging) [F149], [F150]
- Cool cooked food quickly and refrigerate it within 2 hours in shallow, unstacked containers; chill rice ideally within 1 hour. (strong) [F151], [F152], [F153], [F154], [F155]
- Eat batch food from the fridge within 2 days (rice within 1 day) and freeze the rest the same day; the Monday-Tuesday fridge, Wednesday-Friday freezer split is a FightLab convention. (moderate) [F152], [F156], [F157], [F153], [F158]
- Do not freeze potatoes, mayonnaise, fried food, eggs in shell or yogurt and single-cream sauces; cool food in the fridge before freezing it at -18 °C. (moderate) [F158], [F159]
- Defrost in the fridge, starting the night before; never at room temperature; use within 24 hours. (strong) [F158], [F152]
- Reheat until steaming hot throughout, at least 74 °C; reheat rice and refrozen cooked food only once. (strong) [F151], [F153], [F152], [F157]
- Check air-fried meat with a thermometer: poultry 74 °C, whole cuts of pork 63 °C with a 3-minute rest, minced meat 71 °C. (moderate) [F160], [F157], [F161]
- Label each container with the dish and the date, use the oldest first, and freeze in portions. (moderate) [F158], [F152], [F159]

## Planning the session

### Order the batch session from longest to shortest, in parallel by appliance
**Rule:** In a batch session, start what takes longest (pulses, stews, stocks), then the rest, and finish with the quick jobs (dressings, chopping); keep the oven working while the hob cooks, and wash up during the waiting times. FightLab builds the session timeline this way: at each moment it starts, among the steps that are ready, the one whose dish has the most cooking left, on the first free appliance of its kind, and only with the appliances in the user's kitchen profile. The scheduling method, the burners shared by every hob utensil and the substitutions when an appliance is missing (pot for pressure cooker at three times the time, oven for air fryer at 1.25 times, frying pan and wok for each other) are FightLab conventions, and the screen marks a substituted step.
**Evidence:** Two Spanish batch-cooking guides give the same order. La Abuela Carmen (undated brand blog) starts with the longest cooking (pulses, stews, roasts), goes on to sautés, creams and sauces, and ends with quick tasks such as dressings and chopping. TuMenuSemanal (undated blog) puts pulses and stocks on first because they take longest, bakes vegetables and proteins in the oven at the same time, cooks grains on the hob while the oven runs and washes utensils during the waiting times; it mentions the pressure cooker only as an option that shortens chickpeas from about 90 to 25 minutes. Neither guide gives a method for running several appliances at once or for packing during dead time.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Home cooks preparing several dishes for the week in one session. The guides are written for Spanish home kitchens; no study measured the order.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** These are cooking guides, not evidence: no source tests one order against another. Cooking times depend on the recipe, the amount and the appliance, so the timeline is a plan to follow with judgement, not a prescription. A pressure cooker's manual governs its maximum fill, which this file does not state.
**Sources:** [F149], [F150]

## Storing and freezing

### Cool cooked food and refrigerate it within 2 hours, rice within 1 hour
**Rule:** After the session, cool the food quickly and put it in the fridge within 2 hours of cooking: split it into portions, use shallow containers (5 cm deep at most) and do not stack them, so the air circulates. Chill cooked rice as quickly as possible, ideally within 1 hour. The fridge should be at 0-5 °C.
**Evidence:** AESAN's factsheet on Bacillus cereus (2026) says to refrigerate cooked food and leftovers straight away or, if that is not possible, not to leave them more than 2 hours at room temperature, and not to stack containers, so that air circulates and cooling is even; cold food above 5 °C is a risk. The FSA (UK, how to chill, freeze and defrost food safely) says to cool cooked food and refrigerate it within 1-2 hours, to split it into smaller portions or containers so it cools faster and to keep the fridge at 0-5 °C. The FSA's home food fact checker says to chill rice as quickly as possible, ideally within an hour, split into smaller portions. FSAI's Bacillus cereus factsheet (2016) says to cool food as fast as possible so it can be refrigerated within 2 hours, to keep cold food at 5 °C or less and that cooked rice needs rapid cooling. EROSKI Consumer (2022) advises storing within 2 hours (less in hot weather) in shallow lidded containers no more than 5 cm deep, splitting large amounts, below 5 °C.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Cooked food at home. The agencies' advice is general food hygiene, not tied to a population.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** AESAN says to refrigerate immediately, while the FSA says not to put hot food straight into the fridge but to cool it first; both set the same 2-hour limit. FightLab follows the 2-hour limit and, for rice, the FSA's 1 hour. In hot weather the limit is shorter (EROSKI Consumer).
**Sources:** [F151], [F152], [F153], [F154], [F155]

### Eat from the fridge within 2 days and freeze the rest the same day
**Rule:** Batch food eaten in the 2 days after the session comes from the fridge; anything eaten later is frozen on the day it is cooked and thawed the night before (see "Defrost in the fridge the night before"). Cooked rice stays in the fridge for 1 day at most. With a Sunday session this means Monday and Tuesday from the fridge and Wednesday to Friday from the freezer; that split, and the 2-day and 1-day limits as applied by the app, are FightLab conventions built on the most cautious agency advice.
**Evidence:** The FSA says to eat leftovers within 48 hours or freeze them, as soon as possible. OCU (Spain, 2016, on school lunchboxes) says a prepared dish should not stay more than 48 hours in the fridge and should otherwise be frozen, in portions. The FSA's home food fact checker says to keep cooked rice in the fridge for no more than a day. AESAN's freezing infographic (2021) says to freeze leftovers on the same day when too much has been cooked. The USDA (Leftovers and Food Safety, 2012) allows leftovers 3-4 days in the fridge and 3-4 months in the freezer.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Cooked dishes stored at 0-5 °C at home.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The agencies disagree on fridge time: 48 hours (FSA, OCU) against 3-4 days (USDA). EROSKI Consumer gives 3-4 days for stews and 4-6 days for rice, which contradicts the FSA's 1 day for rice; FightLab follows the FSA. The limits are about safety, not quality: frozen food stays safe but slowly loses quality.
**Sources:** [F152], [F156], [F157], [F153], [F158]

### Do not freeze potatoes, mayonnaise, fried food or yogurt sauces
**Rule:** Do not freeze dishes that rely on potatoes, eggs in shell, very fatty food, mayonnaise, fried or breaded food, or vegetables meant to be eaten raw: they lose texture or flavour. Freeze curries and sauces without yogurt or single cream and add those after reheating. Cool food in the fridge before it goes into the freezer, and freeze at -18 °C or below. When a dish the app schedules for the freezer names one of these foods, the menu flags it; the word list behind the flag is a FightLab convention.
**Evidence:** AESAN's freezing infographic lists foods not to freeze because they lose texture and flavour (not for safety): vegetables and fruit eaten raw, potatoes, eggs in shell, high-fat foods, mayonnaise and fried foods. It says not to put hot food straight into the freezer but to cool it in the fridge first, and to keep food at a constant -18 °C or below (a four-star freezer). Nigella Lawson's answer on freezing curry (2011) says yogurt and single cream are likely to split when frozen, so add them after reheating; double cream freezes well, and coconut milk may separate slightly but is fine to eat.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Home freezing of cooked dishes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** These are quality limits, not safety limits: the food is safe, it just tastes or feels worse. A stew with potatoes can be eaten from the fridge, or frozen without the potatoes. OCU also notes that potato and tomato do not freeze well.
**Sources:** [F158], [F159]

### Defrost in the fridge the night before
**Rule:** Move tomorrow's frozen portion to the coldest part of the fridge the night before, on a tray or in a container that catches the liquid, and eat it within 24 hours of thawing. Never thaw at room temperature. If it has to be thawed in the microwave, use the lowest power and cook or reheat it straight away. Do not refreeze thawed food unless it has been cooked above 70 °C for at least 2 minutes. FightLab reminds the user at 22:00 the evening before; the reminder hour is a FightLab convention.
**Evidence:** AESAN's freezing infographic says to thaw in the coldest part of the fridge, taking the food out at least 12 hours ahead and on a tray that catches the liquid; not to thaw at room temperature, because microbes grow faster between 5 and 65 °C; to thaw in the microwave at the lowest power and cook at once; and not to refreeze thawed food unless it is cooked above 70 °C for at least 2 minutes. The FSA says thawing in the fridge, overnight, is safest, in a container that catches drips; the microwave only if the fridge is not possible, then cook immediately; and to use food within 24 hours of it fully defrosting.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Frozen cooked dishes and raw ingredients at home.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Large or dense portions may need more than one night; AESAN's 12 hours is a minimum.
**Sources:** [F158], [F152]

### Reheat until steaming hot throughout and reheat rice only once
**Rule:** Reheat batch food until it is steaming hot all the way through, at least 74 °C in the centre if there is a thermometer, and bring soups and sauces to the boil. Reheat rice only once, and a cooked dish that has been frozen, thawed and refrozen only once more. FightLab asks for each batch portion to be reheated once; extending "only once" to every dish is a FightLab convention, stricter than the sources.
**Evidence:** AESAN's Bacillus cereus factsheet says to reheat to at least 74 °C for at least 15 seconds and that the bacterium's emetic toxin is not destroyed by reheating, so prevention lies in cooling and storage. The USDA says to reheat leftovers to 165 °F (74 °C) measured with a thermometer and to bring sauces and soups to a rolling boil. The FSA's home food fact checker says to reheat until steaming hot all the way through, never to reheat rice more than once, and to reheat previously cooked and frozen meat only once. The FSA's chilling and freezing guidance says cooked food can be refrozen but can then be reheated only once.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Reheating cooked dishes at home, in any appliance.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Neither FSA page opened gives a general "reheat any leftover only once" rule, and the USDA allows refreezing leftovers after reheating them to 165 °F, which is looser than the FSA. Reheating does not make badly cooled food safe: the rice toxin survives heat (AESAN).
**Sources:** [F151], [F153], [F152], [F157]

### Check air-fried meat with a thermometer
**Rule:** When meat is cooked in an air fryer (or oven) in the session or for dinner, check the centre with a food thermometer, because browning on the outside does not show doneness: poultry 74 °C, whole cuts of pork 63 °C followed by a 3-minute rest, and minced meat 71 °C. Do not overfill the basket, and stir or turn halfway.
**Evidence:** Nebraska Extension (University of Nebraska-Lincoln, food safety tips for air fryers, updated 2025) says a food thermometer is the only way to confirm that food is safe, gives poultry 165 °F (73.9 °C), pork and other whole cuts 145 °F (62.8 °C) with a 3-minute rest and ground meat 160 °F (71.1 °C), and warns that an overfilled basket cooks unevenly. The USDA leftovers factsheet gives the same 145 °F with a 3-minute rest, 160 °F and 165 °F. Bosch's quick-start guide for its air fryers says to take the basket out halfway and stir.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Meat cooked at home in air fryers; the temperatures are USDA minimum internal temperatures.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Some cooking guides say to preheat the air fryer, but Bosch says its models need no preheating; the manufacturer's manual governs. The Nebraska page's °C figures for eggs and fish are wrong (fish is given as 73.9 °C for 145 °F), so only the meat figures are used here.
**Sources:** [F160], [F157], [F161]

### Label each container with the dish and the date, first in first out
**Rule:** Portion batch food into individual or meal-size containers, airtight and made for food, and label each one with the dish and the date it was cooked or frozen. In the freezer, what went in first comes out first.
**Evidence:** AESAN's freezing infographic says to mark each container with the food and its freezing date, to use first what went into the freezer first, to freeze in individual or household-size portions, and to use airtight containers or freezer bags made of food-contact material. The FSA says to label frozen food, to use airtight containers or freezer bags and to freeze in individual portions. Nigella Lawson also advises labelling frozen curry with its contents and date.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Home storage of batch-cooked food.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The FSA does not mention first in, first out; AESAN does.
**Sources:** [F158], [F152], [F159]
