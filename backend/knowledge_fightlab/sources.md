### F001
Ding L et al., 2026. Acute:chronic workload ratio and load management for team sports: a multilevel meta-analysis. Front Public Health 14:1896651  
Type: Meta-analysis · DOI: [10.3389/fpubh.2026.1896651](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1896651) · Files: combat-sports  
URL: <https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42662491/>  
Opened: <https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=42662491&rettype=abstract&retmode=text> and <https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=13520966&rettype=xml>  
Supports: Abstract and PMC full text. Of 41 included cohort studies, 16 (797 team-sport athletes) were pooled: elevated ACWR showed a small-to-moderate association with injury (g 0.35, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.54) with I2 95.8%; contact g 0.54, non-contact g 0.55, time-loss g 0.01; most studies of moderate quality; no small-study effects on Egger testing. The authors say observational data cannot show causation or that lowering the ratio lowers injury, and place ACWR as one contextual marker among several, including fatigue and sleep. Rules citing it: ACWR is not a validated injury predictor; ACWR is barely studied in combat sports; The 0.8-1.3 sweet spot is not a safe zone; Do not steer training by the ACWR; Present load as a neutral comparison with the recent average.

### F003
Gabbett TJ, 2016. The training-injury prevention paradox: should athletes be training smarter and harder? Br J Sports Med 50(5):273-280  
Type: Narrative review · DOI: [10.1136/bjsports-2015-095788](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2015-095788) · Files: combat-sports

Opened: <https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pmc&id=4789704&rettype=xml>  
Supports: PMC full text, by the main proponent of the ratio. Presents a 0.8-1.3 lowest-risk band and 1.5 or above as high risk, drawn from cricket, Australian football and rugby league, with a caution for other sports and individual-sport athletes; cricket fast bowlers at 1.5 or more had 2 to 4 times the injury risk over 7 days; advises weekly increases under 10% from unpublished rugby league data (risk under 10% for changes from 5% less to 10% more, 21% to 49% at 15% or more) and an Australian football study (40% of injuries after a weekly change above 10%); chronic load as the rolling average of the last 3 to 6 weeks; more than 18 weeks of prior training linked to lower later injury risk; non-contact soft-tissue injuries halved after a load-threshold prediction model was introduced in rugby league; rationale framed around non-contact soft-tissue injury. Rules citing it: ACWR is barely studied in combat sports; The 0.8-1.3 sweet spot is not a safe zone; Do not steer training by the ACWR; Show the load ratio only after 28 days of history; Present load as a neutral comparison with the recent average.
