# Dropped claims

> Claims considered for FightLab's combat-sports evidence annex that did not make it into combat-sports.md as written, each with the reason. Kept for transparency and as a to-do list: if a source turns up that supports one of these, it can be re-researched. Evidence checked on 2026-10-29.

Tags: `research` = discarded by the researcher (the source could not be opened, the numbers were not in the opened text, or the claim was contradicted, duplicated or out of scope). `verify` = rejected or cut by the independent checker, including parts removed from claims the checker revised and claims folded into another rule. Entries are numbered in order of appearance. Rule names in the reasons are headings in combat-sports.md; sources named here without an ID were not used in the annex.

## ACWR and load spikes

- `FD001` **Limiting weekly load increases to under 10% is a convention with weak supporting data, as a stand-alone rule.** (verify): merged, not refuted. The checker confirmed the numbers in the Gabbett 2016 and Schwellnus 2016 Part 2 full texts, but the rule only labelled a convention, so it was folded into Present load as a neutral comparison with the recent average; the finding that the IOC 10% figure sits in the illness statement and cites no study moved into IOC consensus backs gradual and individual load progression.
- `FD002` **An ACWR of 0.8-1.3 is a safe zone.** (research): no opened source validates a safe band. It comes from Gabbett 2016 and the Blanch and Gabbett cricket, rugby league and Australian football data, with the author's own caution for individual sports; later reviews found 14 binning schemes (Andrade), no derivable minimum-risk threshold (Jiang) and extreme heterogeneity (Ding); the one combat-sport cohort (Lee 2025, taekwondo) went the other way in raw counts. Covered in The 0.8-1.3 sweet spot is not a safe zone as a label to remove; the checker agrees.
- `FD003` **An ACWR above 1.5 doubles, or raises by a stated factor, the risk of injury.** (research): the only numbers are 2 to 4 times in cricket fast bowlers (Gabbett 2016), 2.0 to 4.5 times in a three-cohort critically appraised topic (Myers 2020) and a risk ratio of 2.45 (95% CI 1.48 to 4.06, I2 about 77%) in a six-cohort cricket meta-analysis (Chelladurai 2026, Cureus 18(3):e104847, PMID 41952944, abstract re-opened by the checker, whose authors warn against rigid thresholds). None involves combat sports, all are observational and the method is contested; the checker agrees.
- `FD004` **The ACWR predicts injury in MMA, boxing, judo, wrestling or kickboxing.** (research): there is no ACWR and injury study in these sports. The checker's own PubMed search (12 records) confirms it; the only combat-sport ACWR and injury cohort is in taekwondo (Lee 2025).
- `FD005` **Arguments from Lolli 2019 (two BJSM letters on coupling and scaling), the Lolli 2020 comment on Coyne 2019, the Windt and Gabbett 2019 rebuttal and Gabbett 2020 on debunking myths.** (research): PubMed records exist (PMIDs 29101104, 29899049, 32240976, 30366966) but have no abstract, so the arguments were not opened and no claim is attributed to them; the checker extended this list. In the annex the critic side of the coupling debate rests only on Impellizzeri 2020 and the proponent side only on Coyne 2019.
- `FD006` **Specific wording on load spikes in the IOC Part 1 injury statement.** (research): only the abstract was opened, so only its general point (rapid changes in load are part of the load concept linked to injury) is used. The 10% figure comes from the Part 2 illness statement.
- `FD007` **EWMA is the better way to calculate the ACWR.** (research): the sources conflict (Griffin 2020 and Maupin 2020 favour EWMA for sensitivity; Wang 2020 describes initial-load and taper problems). Recorded as a conflict in Do not steer training by the ACWR and Show the load ratio only after 28 days of history.
- `FD008` **A chronic average needs at least a set number of weeks of data to be valid.** (research): no opened source states a minimum. The 28-day requirement in Show the load ratio only after 28 days of history is a FightLab convention.
- `FD009` **Qin 2025 shows that injury risk is lowest at an ACWR of 0.8-1.3.** (research): the 56% pooled proportion (95% CI 0.14 to 0.94) comes from single-arm cohorts with no reference group, so it is not a risk comparison; used only as a caveat.
- `FD010` **Wrestling and taekwondo wellbeing-load studies (PMIDs 37369136, 37077780, 42051620) as ACWR injury evidence.** (research): they are about load versus recovery or wellbeing, not injury, so no claim was made. The checker also saw a wrestling workload study (PMID 33917536: 10 elite youth wrestlers, weekly ACWR described with no injury outcome), which supports the point that combat sports lack ACWR injury data but adds no claim.

## Making weight and rehydration

- `FD011` **The ACSM weight-making statement rests on limited and mostly laboratory data, as it says itself.** (verify): not in the opened text in that form; replaced with what the statement does say (recovery and performance studies are mostly laboratory based).
- `FD012` **Many judo competitors usually cut 5-10% of body mass (mean 6%) (Artioli 2010).** (verify): a misreading; in the abstract 5-10% (mean 6%) is the largest loss ever, not the usual cut. Corrected in Rapid weight loss is common but not safe because of it.
- `FD013` **Athletes regain about 5-8% of body mass after the weigh-in, as a rule range.** (verify): not a figure from any opened source; replaced with the sourced averages (about 6% pooled, about 8% in professional MMA), which the app may report only as descriptive averages.
- `FD014` **The ACSM statement gives a mean regain of around 10%.** (verify): the text says mean gains of up to 10%; corrected.
- `FD015` **Displaying consensus recovery amounts (per kg, per hour or as a percentage of the loss) in app content.** (verify): this reads as a rehydration protocol, which FightLab must not give. The amounts are kept only in the evidence for traceability, and Post-weigh-in recovery guidance is general consensus only now gives qualitative information plus referral to a sports dietitian.
- `FD016` **Water loading has conflicting evidence.** (verify): the opened sources describe it as limited and early, not conflicting; reworded in Do not promote water loading, diuretics or IV fluids.
- `FD017` **Severe dehydration explains confusion or collapse, and confusion and loss of coordination are signs named by the heat-illness position stand.** (verify): not in the opened position stand abstract; removed, and the examples in Collapse or confusion during a cut is an emergency are labelled FightLab convention.
- `FD018` **Specific post-weigh-in recovery amounts and time windows from Reale, Slater and Burke 2017 (the Eur J Sport Sci full text and the decision tree in their IJSPP 2017 paper).** (research): only abstracts were opened, so no numbers from these papers are cited, and recovery amounts by window (for example under 2 hours, 2 to 8 hours, over 8 hours) were not found in any opened text. The checker notes that such amounts would be a protocol and FightLab would not display them anyway.
- `FD019` **A safe percentage of body mass that can be cut without performance loss (for example 5%).** (research): the opened sources disagree (Mauricio 2022: up to 5% in under 7 days without strength or power effect; Martinez-Aranda 2023: 3-5% with at least 24 hours; Barley 2018 RCT: decrements at 4.8%; the ACSM statement, checked by the checker in the Burke 2021 text: losses of 2-3% likely have few disadvantages and losses above 3% may have substantial negative implications). Not stated as a rule; reported only as conflicting evidence in Performance effects of rapid weight loss are mixed, with no safe percentage.
- `FD020` **The incidence or rate of death or kidney injury from weight cutting.** (research): no opened source gives a denominator; Barley 2019 states that research on heat illness and cardiovascular complications in combat sports is lacking.
- `FD021` **Same-day versus day-before weigh-ins compared for performance in MMA or boxing.** (research): no opened source compares them directly; only indirect evidence appears in A longer weigh-in-to-bout gap is not protective.
- `FD022` **Weight cutting causes brain injury or concussion.** (research): the ACSM statement says empirical data are lacking, and Bray 2021 used weight class, not cutting. Reworded as a hypothesis in Weight cutting affects mood and can distort concussion tests.
- `FD023` **Female-specific cut and regain data beyond Murugappan 2021 (57 women) and Matthews 2019 (156 women).** (research): no opened source gives sex-specific safety or performance findings; Matthews notes few data in women.
- `FD024` **IOC REDs 2023 recommendations specific to weight-category or combat sports.** (research): only the abstract was opened, and it does not mention weight cutting, so No weight-cut content for minors, and refer repeated cutters stays with general REDs.
- `FD025` **Effects of the California commission 10-point plan, the ONE Championship rules and the 5% morning re-weigh on weight-cutting behaviour.** (research): the ACSM statement itself says no impact data exist, and no peer-reviewed evaluation was opened.
- `FD026` **A validated symptom checklist for stopping a cut.** (research): no opened source provides one; only the central nervous system and collapse signs of heatstroke (Armstrong 2007) and the risk domains of the ACSM statement are supported. The wider list in Collapse or confusion during a cut is an emergency is labelled FightLab convention and needs clinician review.
- `FD027` **Participant counts for the Mauricio 2022 and Brechney 2022 meta-analyses.** (research): not in the abstracts opened, so not cited.
- `FD028` **Per-sport prevalence of rapid weight loss in Muay Thai, kickboxing and BJJ beyond the Barley 2018 survey.** (research): only the multi-sport survey abstract gives figures, and it singles out MMA, wrestling and taekwondo; the other sports are not quantified.

## Concussion and head impacts

- `FD029` **A FIS (skiing) reproduction of the CRT6 as the basis for the red-flag wording.** (verify): source removed and replaced by the official CRT6 (BJSM PDF). The FIS file is a modified skiing reproduction whose red-flag list and observation period (1 to 2 hours instead of at least 3 hours) differ from the official tool, so it must not be used as the basis for app wording. No concussion claim was dropped by the checker.
- `FD030` **Hard sparring produces more or bigger head impacts than technical or light sparring.** (research): no opened study compared sparring intensities; the instrumented studies report single sparring conditions or pool sparring and competition. No FightLab rule can lean on a hard-versus-light difference in head-impact dose.
- `FD031` **A numeric limit on sparring rounds, hard-sparring days per week or head contacts per session.** (research): no peer-reviewed source in the opened set sets or tests such a cap. The only dose-related data are cross-sectional (weekly sparring rounds not linked to smaller brain volumes in MMA; rounds correlated with plasma neurofilament light). Any cap would be a FightLab convention.
- `FD032` **A fixed number of days without sparring after a concussion, specific to combat sports.** (research): only the general consensus timeline exists (at least about 1 week, typically within about a month, individualised); no combat-sport-specific time was found.
- `FD033` **Incidence of concussion or head injury in sparring or training in MMA, kickboxing and Muay Thai.** (research): training incidence was found only for amateur boxing (all injuries, 1.3 per 1000 hours). No systematic review of kickboxing or Muay Thai concussion incidence or of MMA training injuries was found, and the 2025 MMA review states that training data are lacking.
- `FD034` **The red-flag list as printed in the Amsterdam 2022 consensus statement itself.** (research): the checker confirmed that the statement has no red-flag list; the list is in the CRT6, which the checker read in its official BJSM form, so a reproduction is no longer the basis.
- `FD035` **Specific figures from the SCAT6, the SCOAT6 and the separate return-to-sport strategy papers.** (research): the full tools were not opened, so only what the consensus statement says about them is used. The checker notes that the return-to-sport systematic review abstract (Putukian 2023) was opened and is now cited for the 19.8 days.
- `FD036` **The concussion share of injuries in kickboxing.** (research): no kickboxing-specific epidemiology source was opened; kickboxing appears only as one category in the US emergency-department data, without a separate figure.
- `FD037` **Headgear reduces concussion in sparring.** (research): contradicted by the opened ringside physicians position statement, which says it is unproven; used as a do-not-claim rule in Do not present headgear as concussion protection.
- `FD038` **Retirement thresholds after repeated concussions.** (research): the consensus says there is no clear evidence for factors that would unequivocally require retirement (confirmed by the checker in the statement); not turned into an app rule.
- `FD039` **Claims from studies of contact breast injuries, taekwondo, karate, judo competition rescue records, blood biomarker and EEG reviews and finite-element simulation.** (research): opened as titles or abstracts only and judged off-topic or too indirect to support a rule; no claims drawn.

## Intensity of combat training

- `FD040` **Use the ACSM bands as the reference for moderate and vigorous, noting that they are not defined in CR10.** (verify): already covered by the base rule "ACSM intensity bands" (cardio-health), built on the full Garber 2011 position stand and graded strong; this claim was a weaker duplicate resting on secondary copies, and its sources (the Garber 2011 abstract, a dog-walking study and a Kravitz summary of the ACSM guidance) are no longer cited. The CR10 point is carried in Technical drilling runs below sparring and Do not class a combat session by mean heart rate alone.
- `FD041` **A table of proposed FightLab modality classes with what the evidence allows.** (verify): a restatement of the other intensity rules with no new evidence, and its row saying that judo uchi-komi can be vigorous went beyond the opened abstract (absolute VO2 only). For the implementer: emerging evidence supports striking sparring as vigorous by HR, fast-pace pad and bag work as vigorous and slow bag rounds as moderate, and karate technical drilling below sparring; any BJJ or wrestling rolling class, any pad or bag default when pace is unknown, any judo drill or randori class and any CR10 cut-off are FightLab conventions.
- `FD042` **Julio 2017 judo energy-system shares as evidence for the class of judo drills.** (verify): the numbers are correct (oxidative 70%, ATP-PCr 21%, glycolytic 8%), but they describe energy-system shares in simulated matches and do not inform the intensity class of any training modality; sex is not reported in the abstract. Removed with its source.
- `FD043` **The Crisafulli 2009 MET value as part of the sparring intensity rule.** (verify): 9.39 METs is in the abstract, but METs belong to the energy section and add nothing to the class decision; removed from the intensity rule (the value is used in the energy section).
- `FD044` **A systematic review or meta-analysis pooling %HRmax, lactate and VO2 of MMA, boxing, BJJ, judo and wrestling matches or sparring.** (research): none found in PubMed searches; the existing reviews (Slimani 2017 on boxing and on RPE, James 2016 on MMA, Chaabene 2015 and 2017, Andreato 2017) are narrative reviews or systematic reviews of fitness profiles whose opened abstracts contain no pooled %HRmax or lactate values.
- `FD045` **Per-sport pooled %HRmax values from the Chaabene 2015 boxing, Slimani 2017 boxing and Franchini 2014 judo reviews.** (research): only abstracts were opened and they hold no HR numbers; the full texts are not in PMC.
- `FD046` **James 2016 MMA review as evidence of match intensity.** (research): the abstract covers fitness differences between levels, not the intensity of training modalities.
- `FD047` **Mean %HRmax or lactate of BJJ rolling by intensity of the roll.** (research): no opened source gave rolling HR values; the 2024 BJJ pilot and Villar 2018 abstracts have no HR numbers.
- `FD048` **The intensity of wrestling live sparring.** (research): no wrestling training study was found, and the opened review abstract has no demand figures.
- `FD049` **MMA training categories by segmented RPE load (Kirk 2023, 20 athletes: warm-up, drills, sparring).** (research): the abstract gives load in AU (33.8 for the warm-up up to 122.8 for BJJ drills) without durations, so intensity cannot be derived.
- `FD050` **Ghoul 2019 simulated MMA competition HR, RPE and lactate.** (research): the abstract describes the demands as high with no numbers.
- `FD051` **The ACSM table columns for CR10, %HRR, VO2R and METs.** (research): the full position stand was not opened by the researcher; now moot, because the base rule "ACSM intensity bands" (cardio-health) carries the full table.
- `FD052` **Training-intensity numbers from the Bridge 2014 taekwondo review and the Chaabene 2012 karate review.** (research): the abstracts hold no training-intensity figures.
- `FD053` **Absolute HRmax by modality for men only in Tabben 2014 karate.** (research): the sex-specific values exist in the paper but are not needed; the checker added the sex difference in %HRmax to the conflicts of Technical drilling runs below sparring.

## Session-RPE, monotony and the PMC

- `FD054` **Foster monotony above 2.0 is a risk threshold.** (research): not in any opened text; the Foster 1998 abstract reports individually identifiable thresholds only, and Thornton 2016 reports a different, sample-specific cut-off (0.78). Dropped as a universal rule; the checker confirmed that no opened source gives 2.0.
- `FD055` **Monotony is unstable when the SD is small, as a published criticism.** (research): no opened paper makes this criticism. Kept only as an arithmetic note, labelled as such, in Monotony and strain are descriptive indices.
- `FD056` **CR10 gives different results from the 6-20 Borg scale for session load.** (research): no head-to-head study was found; Use the CR10 scale with Foster's wording only says that the validation rests on the modified CR10.
- `FD057` **Session-RPE ratings collected hours later, or the next day, are equivalent to 30-minute ratings.** (research): no opened source tested this; only 10 versus 30 minutes (boxers) and 1 versus 30 minutes (youth taekwondo) were found.
- `FD058` **TSB form thresholds (a negative or positive TSB range as optimal or risky).** (research): no scientific source was found or opened.
- `FD059` **CTL 42 days and ATL 7 days as evidence-based constants.** (research): no opened source defines or validates these values; treated as a FightLab convention in The 42-day and 7-day PMC constants are conventions.
- `FD060` **The Banister 1975 original impulse-response paper as a source.** (research): not indexed in PubMed and not opened; only later work is used.
- `FD061` **Any combat-sport study of the fitness-fatigue or PMC model.** (research): searches returned nothing relevant; stated in The fitness-fatigue model predicts individual performance poorly and A PMC display is a labelled FightLab convention.
- `FD062` **Prospective validation of monotony and strain for injury or performance in combat sports.** (research): not found; only observational weekly-pattern studies exist (Combat-sport monotony and strain data are weak and observational).
- `FD063` **The Bourdon 2017 monitoring consensus statement and the Impellizzeri 2019 commentary as sources of rules.** (research): opened by the researcher, but the abstracts contain no specific numeric recommendation relevant to these rules; not cited.
- `FD064` **Haddad 2011b (r = 0.20 and 0.14 in intermittent taekwondo sessions) cited only second-hand.** (research): superseded by the checker, who opened the primary paper (J Hum Kinet 2011;29:59-66, PMC3588613) as full text and confirmed the numbers; it is now cited directly.

## Energy expenditure and PAL

- `FD065` **Exercise kcal do not add linearly to daily needs.** (verify): the numbers are correct, but all the evidence is general-population (Westerterp 2018 and 2004) with no combat-specific data, and the base rule "Exercise calories do not add linearly" (nutrition) already covers it. Westerterp 2018 and 2004 stay in use for Athletes can exceed PAL 2.0, with a ceiling near 2.4-2.5.
- `FD066` **Ogawa 2024 junior sumo data as support for the wrestler PAL rule.** (verify): adolescent boys (age 15), sumo, no PAL reported; the app targets adults and the use made of it (that wrestlers sit more outside training) was weak. Removed with its source.
- `FD067` **The Compendium does not show which combat MET values are measured and which are estimated.** (research): reversed by the checker. The sports page does mark estimated codes in red, and among the combat entries only 15730 (wrestling) is red; this is now included in Use 2024 Compendium METs as combat intensity anchors.
- `FD068` **Measured kcal per minute or per session for BJJ, MMA, judo and wrestling training.** (research): the papers found report energy-system contributions as percentages or laboratory totals, not a session kcal comparable to calorimetry per minute; no MMA or BJJ training-session kcal study was found.
- `FD069` **The accuracy of wearable or HR-based kcal in combat training.** (research): no validation in boxing, MMA, judo, wrestling, BJJ or taekwondo training exists in PubMed; the checker's search returned only a Wii boxing exergame study against an accelerometer. Stated plainly in Wearable kcal are rough and unvalidated in combat sports.
- `FD070` **Twice-daily training justifies a PAL above 2.0, as a tested result.** (research): no opened study compares once-daily and twice-daily training in combat athletes; carried only as a labelled FightLab convention in Athletes can exceed PAL 2.0, with a ceiling near 2.4-2.5 and Collegiate wrestlers measured a PAL near 2.6.
- `FD071` **IOC REDs 2023 energy-availability thresholds (for example 30 kcal/kg FFM).** (research): the full text was not opened; only the abstract is cited, with no numeric thresholds.
- `FD072` **Total daily energy expenditure of taekwondo players from accelerometers (Cho 2013, Cho 2014).** (research): the abstracts give only group differences, not absolute totals.
- `FD073` **The isotope-dilution systematic review of athlete total energy expenditure (Jesus 2025).** (research): no combat-sport breakdown or PAL, so not used.
- `FD074` **The Kung Fu Compendium entry as a combat MET anchor.** (research): the 2024 table lists 15455 Kung Fu Gymnastics at 5.5, which is not a combat-training entry and is not needed for the annex, so it is not cited.
