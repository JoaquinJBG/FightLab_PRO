# Combat sports

> Part of FightLab's evidence annex for combat-sport athletes. Rules, not a literature review. Source IDs refer to sources.md in this folder. This content belongs to FightLab and is licensed CC BY 4.0; it is not part of ai-personal-coach. It was researched with the method of ai-personal-coach's docs/research-methodology.md: a researcher found and opened each source, an independent checker re-opened every source and tried to refute every claim, and anything that could not be opened was dropped (open it or drop it). Evidence checked on 2026-10-29.

## How to read this file

Each rule carries a grade, used as in the coach's knowledge base. **Strong**: a position stand, guideline or consensus statement, or several consistent meta-analyses, agree, so apply the rule by default. **Moderate**: one good meta-analysis, consistent trials, or a consensus or position stand resting on limited data, so apply it and adjust to the person. **Emerging**: few or small studies, mostly observational or mechanistic data, or reviews that disagree, so present it as reasonable practice, not proven fact.

The population is adult combat-sport athletes: MMA, boxing, kickboxing, Muay Thai, Brazilian jiu-jitsu, wrestling and judo, amateur to professional. Most combat-sport studies are small samples of young men, often elite or national level, and many rules borrow from team sports or general sports medicine; the "Applies to" line says who was actually studied, and extrapolations are flagged. "FightLab convention" marks a product number or choice that the app needs but that has no evidence behind it; it is never presented as evidence. FightLab informs and refers: it never diagnoses, never prescribes medical treatment and never prescribes a weight cut or a rehydration protocol.

## Key rules at a glance

- Show the acute:chronic workload ratio only as a descriptive number; never label a value safe or an injury risk, and remove the 0.8-1.3 safe zone from the coach prompt. (emerging) [F001], [F002], [F003]
- Tell fighters that load-ratio research comes from team sports and has barely been tested in combat sports. (emerging) [F004], [F005]
- Progress load gradually and individually, as IOC consensus advises; any numeric weekly-increase flag, such as 10%, is a FightLab convention. (moderate) [F006], [F007]
- Present load as this week against the 4-week average in neutral words, next to how the athlete feels; the 28-day minimum history is a FightLab convention. (emerging) [F001], [F008], [F009]
- On making weight, inform and refer to a sports dietitian or physician; never give a cutting or rehydration protocol, and present planned, gradual weight management as the consensus preference. (moderate) [F010], [F011]
- Rapid weight loss is very common, most of all in MMA, but common does not mean safe. (moderate) [F012], [F013], [F014]
- Weight regained after the weigh-in is not proof of rehydration or readiness, and the app never sets a regain target. (moderate) [F015], [F016]
- Sauna, sweat suits and hot-room work to force weight loss are the methods linked to deaths; collapse or confusion during a cut is a medical emergency. (moderate) [F010], [F017], [F018]
- No weight-cut content for users under 18 (FightLab convention backed by consensus); repeated cuts or long restriction prompt a referral about REDs and eating disorders. (moderate) [F010], [F019], [F020]
- Performance effects of a cut are mixed and no safe percentage exists; the app never states one. (emerging) [F021], [F022], [F023]
- Do not present water loading, diuretics, laxatives, vomiting or IV fluids as options; refer instead. (emerging) [F010], [F024]
- After a suspected head injury: stop training now, no return that day and no contact until a clinician has assessed the athlete; the app never diagnoses. (strong) [F025], [F026]
- Red-flag signs after a head blow replace coaching with an urgent emergency-care message; link to the official CRT6 rather than copy it. (strong) [F026]
- A head blow with any symptom sets no_contact until the athlete confirms clinician clearance (the flag mechanics are a FightLab convention); walking and everyday activity are fine if they do not clearly worsen symptoms. (strong) [F025], [F026], [F027]
- Return to contact follows the six-step graded plan, at least 24 hours per step, with a clinician authorising the contact steps; the app computes no return date. (strong) [F025], [F028]
- Concussion is among the commonest serious injuries in MMA and boxing competition, with rates that vary widely between studies. (moderate) [F029], [F030], [F031]
- Do not claim that headgear prevents concussion; it mainly prevents cuts. (moderate) [F032], [F025]
- Hard striking and MMA sparring is vigorous in the small samples measured; class pad and bag work by pace, and technical drilling below sparring. (emerging) [F033], [F034]
- Do not class a combat session by mean heart rate alone and let RPE stand; any CR10 cut-off and any grappling-roll class are FightLab conventions. (emerging) [F034], [F035], [F036]
- Session-RPE (CR10 x minutes) is a valid general load measure in arbitrary units. (moderate) [F037], [F038], [F039]
- Monotony, strain and the PMC are descriptive only: no risk labels, TSB zones or predictions, and the 42-day and 7-day constants are FightLab conventions. (emerging) [F040], [F041], [F042]
- Use 2024 Compendium METs to class intensity, but show session kcal as an approximate range, and treat wearable kcal as rough. (moderate) [F043], [F044]
- Use the FAO/WHO/UNU PAL bands (1.40-1.69, 1.70-1.99, 2.00-2.40) as the reference scale. (strong) [F045]
- Do not cap athlete PAL at 2.0: hard training reaches about 2.0-2.4, and sustained values above about 2.4-2.5 need a measured basis. (moderate) [F046], [F047], [F048]
- On low energy availability, inform and refer; never lower an intake target because of a rough kcal estimate. (emerging) [F049], [F050]

## ACWR and load spikes

### ACWR is not a validated injury predictor
**Rule:** FightLab may show the ratio of this week's load to the recent average as a descriptive monitoring number. It must not present it as a validated predictor of injury and must not attach a "safe" or "injury risk" label to any value. Coach text must say that the research behind ratio thresholds comes mostly from team sports and is disputed.
**Evidence:** Two meta-analyses and several systematic reviews of mostly team-sport cohorts find some association but disagree on how useful it is. Ding 2026 (multilevel meta-analysis: 41 cohort studies met inclusion, 16 studies with 797 team-sport athletes pooled) found a small-to-moderate pooled association between elevated ACWR and injury (Hedges g 0.35, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.54) with extreme heterogeneity (I2 95.8%). Contact (g 0.54) and non-contact (g 0.55) injuries showed associations, while time-loss injury outcomes were near null (g 0.01, 95% CI -0.34 to 0.37). 80% of the 41 studies were rated moderate quality, and Egger's test showed no small-study effects. The authors conclude that ACWR is not supported as a stand-alone causal or predictive model and that observational data cannot show that lowering the ratio lowers injury. Qin 2025 (22 single-arm cohorts, 16 high and 6 moderate quality) found ACWR associated with injury but advised caution because of heterogeneity, possible publication bias and differing calculation methods. Andrade 2020 (20 studies, 2375 injuries, 1234 male athletes, mean age 24) found that most studies report higher time-loss injury risk at a higher ACWR than at a low or moderate one, but methods varied too much for firm recommendations (14 binning schemes, almost none shared). Griffin 2020 (22 cohort studies, median Newcastle-Ottawa score 8, range 5 to 9) supported an association with non-contact injury and use as one part of a wider monitoring system. Maupin 2020 (27 studies, quality 48.2% to 64.3%) found high variability and said method problems must be fixed before confident use. Jiang 2022 (professional male soccer) found session-RPE ACWR possibly linked to non-contact injury but could not derive a minimum-risk threshold. Wang 2021 (12 elite male football studies) rated all studies poor quality.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Team-sport athletes, mostly elite or professional adults (soccer, rugby, Australian football, cricket and others). Andrade 2020, Wang 2021 and Jiang 2022 included only men; sex is not reported in the Ding 2026 text opened. Griffin 2020 calls for data in both sexes. None of these reviews includes combat sports.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The reviews agree on some association but not on usefulness. Griffin 2020, Maupin 2020 (trend to lowest risk at 0.80-1.30) and Andrade 2020 (co-authored by Blanch and Gabbett, the concept's main proponents) lean supportive; Ding 2026, Qin 2025 and Wang 2021 stress heterogeneity and poor quality; Impellizzeri 2020 finds no evidence for using ACWR to manage load for injury reduction (see "Do not steer training by the ACWR"). Qin 2025 flags possible publication bias, while Ding 2026 found no small-study effects. All data are observational.
**Sources:** [F001], [F051], [F009], [F008], [F052], [F053], [F054]

### ACWR is barely studied in combat sports
**Rule:** FightLab must not tell combat-sport athletes that ACWR bands are validated for their sport. Where the app shows the ratio to a boxer, MMA fighter, judoka or similar, it should say the ratio comes from team-sport research and has barely been tested in combat sports.
**Evidence:** The only combat-sport ACWR and injury data found is a prospective descriptive cohort in collegiate taekwondo (Lee 2025: 110 players over the 2020-2022 seasons, 841 injuries, workload measured as training and competition duration, ACWR by rolling average and EWMA). The one systematic review of load and injury in a combat sport (Hu 2026, taekwondo: 21 studies, 3248 athletes, 18 on external load and 3 on internal load) lists Lee's ACWR result in its study table but does not grade ACWR as a risk factor; it rates rapid weight loss as strong evidence, competition exposure and training frequency and duration as moderate, and asks for more high-quality prospective studies. A PubMed search run during verification (ACWR terms combined with boxing, MMA, judo, wrestling, kickboxing, Muay Thai, jiu-jitsu, karate, taekwondo or combat sport) returned 12 records; apart from Lee 2025, none studied ACWR and injury in a combat sport (one described weekly ACWR in 10 elite youth wrestlers without injury outcomes). Gabbett 2016 frames rapid load increases as a cause of much non-contact soft-tissue injury. A scoping review on back problems (Zemkova 2020, 48 articles) found limited evidence linking ACWR to back pain or non-contact back injury in individual and team sports and suggested poorly specified time windows as one reason.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Taekwondo only: 110 collegiate players, 56 women and 54 men in the PMC version (the PubMed abstract says 58 women) (Lee 2025); a 21-study taekwondo review mixing elite (1138), non-elite (1864) and mixed (246) athletes, youth and adult (Hu 2026). Nothing for MMA, boxing, kickboxing, judo, wrestling or BJJ.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Applying team-sport findings to combat sports is an assumption. It is tempting to argue that the ratio matters less where many injuries come from contact (strikes, throws), because Gabbett 2016 frames the rationale around non-contact injury, but the newest team-sport meta-analysis found similar pooled associations for contact (g 0.54) and non-contact (g 0.55) injuries (Ding 2026). FightLab should therefore claim neither that the ratio is relevant to combat-sport injuries nor that it is irrelevant to contact injuries.
**Sources:** [F004], [F005], [F003], [F055], [F001]

### The 0.8-1.3 sweet spot is not a safe zone
**Rule:** Remove the text "safe zone 0.8-1.3; above it, injury risk" from the coach prompt, and remove traffic-light risk labels. Never describe any ACWR value as safe or as an injury risk. If bands are kept for display, label them "FightLab convention, borrowed from team-sport research, not a safety threshold" and use neutral colours.
**Evidence:** Gabbett 2016, a narrative review by the concept's main proponent, presents a guide drawn from Blanch and Gabbett's cricket, Australian football and rugby league data in which 0.8-1.3 is the sweet spot and 1.5 or above the danger zone, and advises keeping the ratio near 0.8-1.3 to minimise injury. The same text says different sports may have different load-injury relationships and that applying the guide to individual-sport athletes needs caution. It reports that in cricket fast bowlers a ratio of 1.5 or more went with 2 to 4 times greater injury risk over the next 7 days. Blanch and Gabbett 2016 report a polynomial relationship between ACWR and injury likelihood (R2 0.53) in the same three sports; the paper carries a 2019 erratum. Later reviews do not confirm a validated band: Maupin 2020 saw only a trend toward lowest risk at 0.80-1.30 while flagging different reference groups (0.80-1.20 versus 0.85 or below) and high-ratio cut points (1.50-1.80 versus 1.50 or above); Andrade 2020 found 14 binning schemes; Jiang 2022 could not derive a minimum-risk threshold; Ding 2026 found extreme heterogeneity (I2 95.8%). Qin 2025 reported the lowest pooled injury proportion in the 0.8-1.3 band (56%, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.94), but from single-arm data with no comparison group, which cannot support a safe label. In the only combat-sport cohort (Lee 2025, taekwondo), most injuries were recorded in the 0.8-1.3 band (traumatic: 45% by rolling average, 52% by EWMA; gradual-onset: 35% and 49%), and the authors called it a danger zone for taekwondo. The opened text gives no time spent in each band, so these counts are not risks and neither confirm nor refute the band; they do show that the team-sport band did not transfer cleanly.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Origin data: elite team-sport players in cricket, rugby league and Australian football (sex not stated in the opened texts). Taekwondo data: 110 collegiate players, 56 women and 54 men.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Gabbett 2016 and Maupin 2020 describe 0.8-1.3 as lowest risk and Qin 2025 finds the lowest pooled proportion there; Lee 2025 reports the opposite reading in taekwondo; Jiang 2022 and Ding 2026 find no usable threshold. The sweet-spot idea is popular practice outrunning the evidence.
**Sources:** [F003], [F056], [F052], [F009], [F053], [F001], [F051], [F004]

### Do not steer training by the ACWR
**Rule:** Never word ACWR output as a cause of injury or as a target to steer toward. Use descriptive wording only, and state in the coach text that the ratio is a rough descriptor with known statistical problems.
**Evidence:** Impellizzeri 2020 (IJSPP) argues that advising athletes to manipulate a prognostic factor assumes a causal effect, that no study has tried to estimate that effect properly, and that the ratio is inaccurate (it fails to normalise the numerator by the denominator even when uncoupled), ambiguous and not consistently or unidirectionally related to injury; it concludes there is no evidence for using ACWR in load management aimed at reducing injury. The same group's two-part JAT 2020 commentary lists ten pitfalls (exposure measures, ratios, load measures, time windows, discretisation and reference category, injury definitions, unclear analyses, sample size, missing data, reporting) and argues that no evidence supports using load data quantitatively to steer training to prevent injury. Their two-part JOSPT 2020 commentary adds that the lack of a causal framework invites p-hacking and confirmation bias, and offers avoiding too much training too soon as a common-sense rule based on training principles rather than on study confirmation. Wang 2020 (Sports Med) argues that a ratio may not represent change in activity well, that EWMA ACWR has an initial-load problem and does not suit sports with tapering, and lists discretisation, sparse data, bias in injured athletes and time-varying confounding as analysis problems. Ding 2026 states that its observational evidence cannot show that elevated ACWR causes injury or that reducing it would reduce injury. On the proponent side, Gabbett 2016 reports that non-contact soft-tissue injuries halved in elite rugby league after an injury-prediction model based on training-load thresholds was introduced; this is an uncontrolled before-after report and the model was not the ACWR. On mathematical coupling, Coyne 2019 (international-level basketball and weightlifting athletes) found coupled and uncoupled ACWR correlated at r .88 to .99 with almost all differences trivial (g 0.04 to 0.21) and concluded coupling may matter little; Impellizzeri 2020 argues the ratio is flawed even when uncoupled.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Methodological commentaries based on the team-sport literature; Coyne 2019 is a small case study of two international-level groups (basketball, weightlifting); Gabbett's before-after report comes from one elite rugby league setting.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Direct disagreement. Critics (the Impellizzeri group, Wang 2020) versus proponents (Gabbett 2016 and Blanch 2016; Griffin 2020, which rated EWMA more sensitive; Maupin 2020, which also suggests EWMA may be more sensitive; Coyne 2019 on coupling). Wang 2020 raises specific problems with EWMA, so the idea that EWMA fixes the ratio is not settled. Coupling remains debated; critiques by Lolli and colleagues, including a published comment on Coyne 2019, could not be opened beyond their titles and are not used.
**Sources:** [F002], [F057], [F058], [F059], [F060], [F061], [F001], [F003], [F062], [F008], [F052]

### IOC consensus backs gradual and individual load progression
**Rule:** FightLab may say that the IOC consensus on load and injury counts poor load management, including rapid changes in training and competition load, among risk factors for injury, and that the companion IOC statement on illness recommends monitoring load and making individualised, small increases to limit the risk of illness. It must not claim that either statement defines a safe ACWR or a validated weekly-increase threshold for injury. Any numeric weekly-increase flag is a FightLab convention (see "Present load as a neutral comparison with the recent average").
**Evidence:** The IOC injury statement (Soligard 2016, Part 1; abstract opened) reports that emerging evidence points to poor load management as a major injury risk factor, defines load broadly to include rapid changes in training and competition load, calendar congestion, psychological load and travel, and gives guidelines for prescribing and monitoring load. The IOC illness and overtraining statement (Schwellnus 2016, Part 2; full text opened) notes that load-illness data come from a few sports only, then recommends an individual training plan, monitoring with external and internal load measures, individualised changes because people differ widely in how fast they adapt, small increments, monitoring competition load and planned recovery. Within this illness guidance it states that injury literature points to weekly increments below 10%, without citing a study at that point. ACWR is not named anywhere in the Part 2 text, and the Part 1 abstract gives no ACWR threshold.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Elite athletes across Olympic sports; expert consensus resting on limited, mostly observational data, described by the authors as emerging (Part 1) and limited to a few sports (Part 2). Not combat-sport specific.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Impellizzeri 2020 (JOSPT Part 2) argues that this kind of guidance rests on weak, confirmation-prone evidence and that avoiding too much too soon is justified by training principles, not by study results. The 10% figure appears in an illness statement and is attributed to unnamed injury literature. Wang 2020 states that the IOC recommended ACWR and thresholds; this could not be checked because the Part 1 full text was not opened. Gabbett, the main ACWR proponent, co-authored both IOC statements.
**Sources:** [F006], [F007], [F060], [F061]

### Show the load ratio only after 28 days of history
**Rule:** Show the load comparison only once the athlete has 28 days of logged sessions; before that, show "not enough history yet". The 28-day requirement is a FightLab convention, not an evidence-based minimum. When logging has gaps, after a layoff (injury or illness) or during a taper, show the comparison only with a caveat that the number may mislead.
**Evidence:** A 28-day chronic window matches the most common research setup (1:4 weekly blocks and the coupled method in 95% of 20 studies in Andrade 2020), but windows vary even among proponents: Gabbett 2016 describes chronic load as the rolling average of the last 3 to 6 weeks, and Wang 2021 found 1:2, 1:3 and 1:4-week windows in football studies. Impellizzeri 2020 (JAT Part 2) lists time windows and missing data among ten pitfalls; Griffin 2020 says the best acute and chronic periods may depend on the sport; Zemkova 2020 attributes weak back-injury findings partly to poorly specified windows. Blanch 2016 notes that a ratio spike can come from load falling away during recovery from injury, not only from an unusually heavy week. Wang 2020 reports that EWMA ACWR has an initial-load problem and does not suit sports with tapering. None of the opened sources states how many weeks of data a chronic average needs.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Team-sport research; combat-sport camp and taper cycles and irregular weeks were not studied.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Rolling average versus EWMA is unsettled (Griffin 2020 and Maupin 2020 favour EWMA for sensitivity; Wang 2020 criticises its initial-load and taper problems). Wang's taper point is made about EWMA; FightLab's coupled rolling average was not tested there, so the taper caveat is a precaution, not a finding. Gabbett 2016 cites a finding that team-sport athletes with more than 18 weeks of training before a first injury had lower risk of a later one, which suggests longer history matters but does not set a minimum for a ratio.
**Sources:** [F009], [F003], [F054], [F058], [F008], [F055], [F056], [F061], [F052]

### Present load as a neutral comparison with the recent average
**Rule:** Display "this week's load is X% above or below your 4-week average" in neutral language, with no "safe", "danger" or "injury risk" wording and no red-green risk colours, and pair it with how the athlete feels (sleep, soreness, fatigue, mood) and what the camp plan intended. Coach text may say that big jumps over the recent average are worth discussing with a coach or physio, and that nothing here is a diagnosis. Any numeric flag, such as a week-on-week increase above 10% or ratio bands, must be labelled "FightLab convention (a common rule of thumb)", and the app must not say that staying under it prevents injury.
**Evidence:** Ding 2026 concludes that ACWR should be read only as a contextual indicator inside individualised load management that uses several markers, naming fatigue and sleep among them. Griffin 2020 supports it as one tool within a wider monitoring system. Impellizzeri 2020 (JAT Parts 1 and 2, JOSPT Part 2) advises relying on training principles, expert judgement and the athlete's response, and says load measures cannot tell whether changes raise or lower injury risk. Maupin 2020 and Andrade 2020 warn that method variation limits firm recommendations. On the 10% figure: Gabbett 2016 recommends keeping weekly increases under 10% based on unpublished professional rugby league data (injury risk under 10% when weekly load changed from 5% less to 10% more, 21% to 49% when it rose 15% or more) and an Australian football study in which 40% of injuries followed a weekly change above 10%; the IOC illness statement repeats under 10% as coming from injury literature without citing a study; JOSPT Part 2 says such rules rest on general training principles, not on study confirmation. In judo, session RPE was a stable, usable load measure across levels and sexes in mixed sessions (Bromley 2018: 29 athletes at an international camp), which supports the load input but not any injury threshold.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Synthesis of team-sport reviews and commentaries; 10% data from professional rugby league (unpublished) and Australian football; judo RPE from 29 athletes at one international camp.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Proponents argue for using ACWR to quantify risk: Gabbett 2016 and Blanch 2016 (which recommends ACWR in return-to-play decisions), and Myers 2020 (critically appraised topic of 3 cohorts; 2 found 2.0 to 4.5 times greater risk at high versus balanced ACWR; rated the evidence level A). Critics say even descriptive framing can be read as risk advice. The rugby league 10% data were never published. The wording choice is FightLab's design judgement, built on the cautious conclusions of the newest reviews.
**Sources:** [F001], [F008], [F057], [F058], [F060], [F052], [F009], [F003], [F007], [F036], [F063], [F056]

### Taekwondo injury factors are rapid weight loss and competition exposure
**Rule:** FightLab may tell combat-sport athletes that the one systematic review of load and injury in a combat sport (taekwondo) found the most consistent evidence for rapid weight loss, moderate evidence for competition exposure and for training frequency and duration, and did not grade weekly load ratios as a factor. This is information only; weight-cut content must stay informational, give no cutting protocol, and refer to a qualified professional.
**Evidence:** Hu 2026 (systematic review, 21 studies: 12 prospective cohorts, 2 retrospective cohorts, 7 cross-sectional; 3248 athletes) graded rapid weight loss as strong evidence, competition exposure and training frequency and duration as moderate, and technical execution, sleep quality and neuromuscular function as limited. In two elite cohorts it includes (197 and 219 athletes), injury rates during weight-loss periods were higher than in regular training (rate ratios 1.77 and 2.09 in men, 2.0 and 2.59 in women). From the Lee cohort it reports 547.7 injuries per 1000 h in competition versus 15.5 per 1000 h in training (rate ratio 35.32, 95% CI 28.73 to 43.16), based on about 199 h of competition and 47,273 h of training exposure. Lee 2025 gives the same contrast (548 versus 16 per 1000 h).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Taekwondo athletes only: elite, non-elite and mixed, youth (one included cohort aged 6 to 17) and adult, both sexes. The review authors say high-quality studies are few.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Hu's strong grade for rapid weight loss is within taekwondo and comes from observational studies in a best-evidence synthesis, not a meta-analysis or consensus, so FightLab grades it emerging for transfer to other combat sports. The competition rate is per hour from a small exposure. Whether these findings extend to MMA, boxing or judo is untested in the opened sources.
**Sources:** [F005], [F004]

## Making weight and rehydration

### Prefer planned weight management to acute dehydration
**Rule:** When an athlete asks about making weight, the app may say that the main sports-medicine consensus (ACSM, 2021) prefers gradual, planned body mass management and choosing a weight class the athlete can reach without undue physical, nutritional or psychological stress, and that acute dehydration methods carry health and performance costs. The app should refer the athlete to a sports dietitian or sports physician for an individual plan and must not provide a cutting protocol itself.
**Evidence:** The ACSM Expert Consensus Statement (Burke et al. 2021) separates chronic body mass management (weeks to months, aimed at fat mass while keeping fat-free mass) from rapid weight loss (RWL, hours to days, manipulating body water, glycogen and gut contents), and notes that the 1996 ACSM guidance already put the emphasis on chronic practices and a cautious approach to acute ones. Its recommendations for safer practice include: choosing a weight class the athlete can reach safely without undue physical, nutritional or psychological stress; judging class selection on body composition and not body mass alone; keeping the athlete's usual body mass within reach of the class; discouraging any weight making among minors and fully informing adults of the risks; and prohibiting unhealthy methods. It concludes that expert guidance from a sports nutrition professional can help an athlete build a pragmatic, long-term approach. The statement notes that most studies of recovery and performance are laboratory based and may not reflect competition outcomes. Reale et al. (2017, abstract) say that the time between weigh-in and competition decides how much acute loss can be done and reversed, and that severe practices can be hazardous.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Weight-category athletes in general (combat sports including wrestling, judo, taekwondo, boxing and MMA, plus rowing, weightlifting and horse racing); the underlying studies are mostly young adult males. Consensus written by 5 authors; no pooled sample.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The consensus acknowledges that culture and the perceived advantage of competing lower keep RWL in use, that athletes report psychological benefits (identity, focus, a sense of advantage), and that minimal weight may not be the optimal performance weight. Reale 2017 states that, although acute manipulation may impair health and absolute performance, its strategic use can improve competitive success. No trial shows that gradual loss produces better competition outcomes in MMA; the preference rests on health reasoning and limited data. The statement also notes that several rule-based measures are hard to enforce.
**Sources:** [F010], [F011]

### Rapid weight loss is common but not safe because of it
**Rule:** The app may state that rapid weight loss before the weigh-in is common in combat sports (most competitors in surveys report doing it at some point), that MMA athletes report the most frequent and largest acute weight loss, and that being common does not make it safe. It should not use prevalence to normalise or recommend cutting.
**Evidence:** In a questionnaire of 822 Brazilian judo competitors (607 men, 215 women, mean age 19.3), 86% had lost weight to compete. Most usually lost up to 5% of body mass (mean 2.5%); the most ever lost was 2-5% for many, but a large share reported a largest loss of 5-10% (mean 6%). Cuts took about 7 days, about 3 times per season, and began at a mean age of 12.6; elite athletes scored as more aggressive than non-elite (Artioli 2010). An online questionnaire of 637 competitors across BJJ, boxing, judo, MMA, Muay Thai or kickboxing, taekwondo and wrestling found body mass manipulation in all sports, with acute loss most prevalent and largest in MMA. MMA athletes reported the highest use of sauna (76%) and water loading (67%) and the second highest use of rubber or plastic suits (63%); meal skipping was 84% in taekwondo and wrestling versus about 58% in other sports (Barley 2018). A systematic review of 16 studies (4,432 participants, 156 women and 4,276 men; boxing, judo, MMA, taekwondo, wrestling) found the largest changes in two MMA cohorts: loss of about 7.4 kg (about 10% of body mass) and regain of 7.4 kg (11.7%). It flagged a lack of objective data on the size of the loss itself and little data in women (Matthews 2019). The ACSM consensus states that most athletes in weight-category sports use both chronic and acute strategies, and that the long recovery windows in professional boxing and MMA support a culture of extreme manipulation (Burke 2021).
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Amateur to elite competitors, mostly men; judo survey n=822 (26% women); multi-sport survey n=637; systematic review n=4,432 (3.5% women), with only 3 of its 16 studies reporting the loss itself. Survey data are self-reported.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Surveys are self-report and recall based, and samples are volunteers. The review warns that post-weigh-in regain is not a valid proxy for how much was lost. Prevalence figures differ by sport, level and country. No opened source sets a safe limit.
**Sources:** [F012], [F013], [F014], [F010]

### Weight regained after the weigh-in is not proof of recovery
**Rule:** The app may report, as descriptive averages, how much body mass combat athletes typically regain between weigh-in and the bout (the pooled and professional MMA figures in Evidence). It must say that gaining the weight back on the scale is not proof of full rehydration, glycogen restoration or restored performance. It must not set or suggest a regain target for the athlete.
**Evidence:** Official California State Athletic Commission data (Dec 2015 to Jan 2018) on 512 professional MMA athletes (455 men, 57 women): 98% gained body mass between weigh-in and the bout. The mean gain was 5.5 kg (8.1% of body mass), similar in men (8.1%) and women (8.0%), and over a quarter of men and a third of women gained more than 10%. Athletes from leading promotions gained more in absolute but not relative terms (Murugappan 2021). A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of 40 studies pooled regain at 6.23% of body mass (95% CI 3.74-8.71; k=11; n=1504; I2=99.2%), or 3.71 kg. In 5 matched cohorts (n=179), the loss was 8.33% and the median regain was 106.5% of the loss (range 63.2-154.1%). Mean urine specific gravity at the official weigh-in was 1.029 (95% CI 1.025-1.033; k=3; n=58), which means concentrated urine. Post-recovery hydration data were too sparse to show normalisation. The authors concluded that regain should not be read as complete rehydration or restored readiness, that the pooled values are descriptive averages and not targets, and that a high regain target could push athletes to cut harder (Sun 2026). Matthews (2019) reports that the time allowed for regain ranged from 3 to 32 hours and that regain size depended on sport, competition structure and recovery time. The ACSM statement notes that MMA and professional boxing allow up to 32 hours, with mean gains of up to 10% and individual gains of up to 20% of body mass.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Professional MMA athletes (California commission data, 89% male) and mixed combat-sport cohorts in the meta-analysis (age, sex and level vary by study). In the meta-analysis, no study was at low risk of bias across all domains (14 some concerns, 21 moderate to serious, 3 serious, 2 with serious limitations).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Very high heterogeneity (I2 about 99%). The commission study has no pre-cut weights, so it cannot separate cut size from refeeding. Most meta-analysis data are field observations with self-selected recovery, and the matched cohorts used non-equivalent observation windows. Hydration evidence after recovery is thin: the meta-analysis had only two single-study post-recovery urine specific gravity time points, and one wrestling study in the Lakicevic 2021 review found urine specific gravity back to normal within 16 hours. So the statement that regain is not proof of rehydration expresses uncertainty; it is not proof that athletes stay dehydrated.
**Sources:** [F016], [F015], [F014], [F010], [F064]

### Dehydration-based cuts have been linked to deaths
**Rule:** The app may state that extreme weight cutting has been associated with deaths in several combat sports and in wrestling, usually when severe fluid restriction was combined with heat exposure or heavy sweating, and that the real frequency is not known. Content should always pair this with a referral to a physician and with a firm statement that sauna, sweat suits and hot-room training to force weight loss are the methods linked to the worst outcomes.
**Evidence:** The ACSM statement says deaths linked to making weight have been reported in taekwondo, college wrestling, MMA, judo, Muay Thai and lightweight rowing, usually through cardiovascular complications and/or heat stroke after severe fluid restriction, passive heat exposure or strenuous sweat-inducing exercise; it describes plasma volume loss with cardiovascular strain and impaired thermoregulation in the heat, possible electrolyte disturbance and heat cramps. A CDC investigation (MMWR 1998) of three previously healthy collegiate wrestlers who died in November and December 1997 found that all three used a similar regimen in the hours before weigh-in (food and fluid restriction, vapour-impermeable suits under warm-ups, vigorous exercise in hot environments) and developed hyperthermia and dehydration. A narrative review (Barley 2019) says heat stroke risk rises with the thermal exposure used for dehydration and that, while several athlete deaths have occurred, research on cardiovascular complications and heat illness in combat sports is lacking.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Case reports and investigations in wrestlers, taekwondo, MMA, judo, Muay Thai and rowers; the CDC report covers 3 male collegiate wrestlers. No population denominator, so no rate can be given.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Evidence is case-based and cannot give risk per cut; the consensus and the CDC report agree on mechanism. Deaths cluster in extreme methods, so the app must not imply that moderate fluid or food manipulation carries the same risk or is proven safe.
**Sources:** [F010], [F017], [F024]

### Kidney strain markers rise after rapid weight loss
**Rule:** The app may say that studies of combat athletes show markers of kidney strain (raised creatinine and urea nitrogen) and concentrated urine after rapid weight loss, and that acute kidney injury has been associated with extreme fluid loss, but that the real risk per cut is unknown. It must not diagnose. FightLab convention (not from the sources, for clinician review): tell anyone with very dark or scant urine, severe flank or back pain, swelling, or inability to pass urine to see a doctor promptly.
**Evidence:** A review of studies in healthy combat athletes who lost about 5% of body mass within about a week (Lakicevic 2021; 10 studies, 171 participants, Muay Thai, wrestling, taekwondo, MMA and judo) found that creatinine, blood urea nitrogen and urine specific gravity rose significantly after RWL in most included studies. The authors read this as dehydration with acute kidney damage. One included study was a single elite MMA athlete whose creatinine rose by about 50% during the final acute cut of an 18% loss, which that case report judged consistent with acute kidney injury. In another, a wrestling study, urine specific gravity returned to normal within 16 hours of recovery. Only 3 of the 10 studies came from the systematic search; the other 7 were added on expert suggestion. The ACSM statement says acute renal stress or injury has been associated with extreme fluid loss during RWL in some weight-category athletes.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Healthy combat athletes, mostly young men with some women (one study 8 women); 10 small studies, 171 participants in total, including one single-athlete case report. Outcomes are biomarker changes, not diagnosed injury, except the case report.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Creatinine and urea rise with dehydration itself and may normalise with rehydration, so a biomarker rise is not the same as kidney damage. The review's damage wording goes beyond its biomarkers, its study selection was only partly systematic, and the included studies differ widely in cut duration and method. No study followed kidney function long term. The urine symptom list in the rule is a FightLab convention, not taken from the sources.
**Sources:** [F064], [F010]

### Weight cutting affects mood and can distort concussion tests
**Rule:** The app may say that weight cutting is consistently associated with worse mood and transiently worse thinking, and that dehydration can make concussion screening tests look worse. It may say that an increased risk of brain injury from cutting is plausible but unproven, and must not state it as fact. After any head blow, the planned no-contact rule applies regardless of hydration or weight status.
**Evidence:** The ACSM statement reports findings that agree across studies: lower vigour and higher anxiety, anger, fatigue, depression and confusion during weight making. It also reports transient, reversible cognitive impairment in some athletes during active weight making, wrestlers scoring worse on concussion screening when dehydrated than at a euhydrated baseline, and variation in non-concussed boxers' concussion test scores partly explained by dehydration. It states that empirical data are lacking to confirm an increased risk of brain injury with dehydration. An observational MRI study of 53 boxers and 103 MMA fighters (Bray 2021) found that lightweights had larger per-fight reductions in some regional brain volumes than heavyweights; the authors only speculate that this might relate to weight cutting, which they did not measure. A narrative review (Barley 2019) describes reduced cushioning of the brain with dehydration as a proposed mechanism.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Mixed wrestlers, boxers and MMA fighters, mostly men; Bray 2021 n=156 professional fighters, MRI and neuropsychological testing, with weight class (not cutting) as the exposure.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Weight class is a poor proxy for weight cutting, so Bray 2021 cannot support a cutting effect. In the same study, heavyweights showed greater yearly declines in regional brain volume and in processing speed and reaction tests, so the pattern is mixed. The consensus says the injury risk is unconfirmed. Mood effects come from a few small studies with self-report measures.
**Sources:** [F010], [F065], [F024]

### Performance effects of rapid weight loss are mixed, with no safe percentage
**Rule:** The app may say that some studies of combat athletes find small declines in maximal strength and repeated high-intensity efforts straight after a rapid cut while others find none, that effects after rehydration are mixed and depend on cut size, method and recovery time, and that no safe percentage is established. It must not tell an athlete that a cut of a given size will not hurt their performance, and must not present any percentage as a safe limit or target.
**Evidence:** A meta-analysis (Brechney 2022) compared performance before RWL, straight after RWL, and 3-36 hours after RWL with regain. Straight after RWL there were small declines in maximal strength (g=-0.29, 95% CI -0.54 to -0.03) and repeated high-intensity effort (g=-0.37, 95% CI -0.59 to -0.16). Between before-RWL and after-regain, performance overall was not significantly different (g=0.22, 95% CI -0.18 to 0.62), but whether the declines are restored by regain remained unclear. A meta-analysis of Olympic combat sports (Mauricio 2022; 10 articles scoring above 6 on TESTEX) found no effect on strength and power of RWL up to 5% of body mass in under 7 days. A systematic review of 16 studies (Martinez-Aranda 2023; n=184 combat athletes with at least 3-4 years of practice and RWL experience) found 6 studies with about 5% loss and no performance change, while 10 studies with 3-6% or more reported worse perceived fatigue, mood, strength, power, hormonal and blood markers or technique kinematics; those authors suggested loss should not exceed 3% to 5% of body mass, with at least 24 hours for recovery. In a randomised crossover of 14 MMA athletes (age 23 plus or minus 4), 4.8% dehydration (3 hours of cycling at 40 C) slowed repeated sled push times by 19% at 3 hours and 14% at 24 hours versus control, despite free eating and drinking afterwards; it also weakened handgrip at 3 hours and shortened the medicine-ball throw at 24 hours, with vertical jump unchanged (Barley 2018 RCT). Real competition results are mixed: the ACSM statement reports that athletes with the greatest apparent weight manipulation were less, equally or more successful than others in different studies. These percentages are recorded for traceability only.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Mostly young male combat athletes (judo, wrestling, taekwondo, boxing, MMA) in laboratory or simulated conditions; the largest single trial here has n=14; review n=184. Women are rarely studied.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The meta-analyses differ: Brechney 2022 found small strength and repeated-effort declines straight after RWL, while Mauricio 2022 found no strength or power effect of up to 5% loss. Both found no clear deficit after recovery, whereas Barley 2018 (RCT) still found repeated-effort deficits at 24 hours, and Martinez-Aranda 2023 found decrements in most studies above 3%. Lab tests (strength, jump, sled push) may not reflect fight performance, and the relative advantage over a lighter opponent is not captured. The 3-5% and 24-hour figures are review authors' interpretation of limited studies and must not be presented by the app as a safe limit or a target.
**Sources:** [F021], [F022], [F066], [F023], [F010]

### A longer weigh-in-to-bout gap is not protective
**Rule:** The app may say that more time after the weigh-in allows more recovery but that, in sports with long gaps, athletes tend to cut and regain more, and that same-day weigh-ins leave little time to recover from a cut. It must not suggest choosing events or timing to enable a bigger cut.
**Evidence:** The ACSM statement says the post-weigh-in period can at least partly offset performance decrements if there is enough time for recovery nutrition. It says sports allowing up to 32 hours (professional boxing, MMA) support a culture of extreme manipulation, with mean gains of up to 10% and individual gains of up to 20% of body mass, and that regain may be linked to recovery duration. It also notes that with a 1-2 hour window, refuelling may be impossible after severe carbohydrate restriction. Matthews (2019) found regain varied with the recovery time allowed (3-32 hours). Barley (2018 RCT, n=14) found repeated-effort performance still impaired 24 hours after 4.8% dehydration. The 2026 meta-analysis (Sun) says a higher regain can reflect either a longer window or a larger preceding cut, and that regulation should address the size of the cut, not only recovery time. A review of the most cited literature (Karnincic 2024) notes that longer recovery periods allow some wrestlers to use aggressive, harmful or prohibited methods. Martinez-Aranda (2023) suggests at least 24 hours for adequate or partial recovery after losses of 3-5%.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Mixed combat sports; no study randomises same-day versus day-before weigh-ins in MMA or boxing. Barley 2018: n=14 male MMA athletes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No direct comparison of recovery windows exists; the ACSM position is an expert interpretation. The performance effect after 24 hours differs between studies (Brechney: not significantly different after regain; Barley: impaired). The 24-hour figure is a review author's recommendation, not a tested threshold.
**Sources:** [F010], [F014], [F023], [F015], [F067], [F066]

### Post-weigh-in recovery guidance is general consensus only
**Rule:** The app may explain, as attributed general information, that sports nutrition consensus (ACSM 2021) describes recovery after the weigh-in in broad terms: replacing more fluid than was lost, including sodium through drinks or salty foods, taking carbohydrate-rich food and fluid in frequent smaller amounts, and protecting gut comfort, with what is achievable depending on the time before the bout. It must say that this is expert consensus largely extrapolated from other athletes and that no recovery approach has been shown to be superior in combat athletes, and it must refer the athlete to a sports dietitian for any individual plan. It must not display fluid, sodium, carbohydrate or protein amounts per kg of body weight or as a percentage of the weight lost, compute amounts from the athlete's own weight or losses, or tie any of this to the weigh-in countdown. It should say that intravenous rehydration is prohibited by sport governing bodies and was no better than drinking in the studies the consensus cites.
**Evidence:** The ACSM statement (Burke 2021) gives general sports nutrition advice for recovery between weigh-in and event: replace more fluid than was lost (about 150% of the deficit) to cover ongoing urine and sweat losses; include sodium (oral rehydration solutions, a stated sodium content, or salty foods), which matters more when the loss came from sweating than from fluid restriction; take a larger first drink followed by smaller regular volumes to keep gastric emptying high; add protein, which may help fluid retention; and take carbohydrate at a high rate in frequent small feedings, using moderate to high glycaemic index foods with some protein. Glycogen restoration is fastest in the first hours after exercise. The specific per-kg and percentage figures in the statement are recorded in this annex only for traceability, not for display as targets. The statement also warns that full recovery may be impossible in a short window, that large volumes can cause gut discomfort that harms performance, and that with a 1-2 hour window refuelling may be insufficient after severe carbohydrate restriction. Intravenous fluids showed no benefit over oral fluids in non-weight-category athletes and are strongly discouraged because sport governing bodies ban them. A narrative review (Barley 2019) gives a similar fluid range (125-150% of fluid lost), adds that this may be unrealistic in short windows because gut discomfort may outweigh the benefit, and says electrolytes matter most after sweat-induced loss. The 2026 meta-analysis of 40 studies could not run any strategy-effect meta-analysis because comparative evidence was too heterogeneous, and it describes existing recommendations as starting points to be adjusted to the athlete.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Derived from sports nutrition literature mostly in endurance and non-weight-category athletes, plus small combat studies; no recovery protocol has been tested head to head in combat athletes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The Burke 2021 and Barley 2019 fluid figures differ slightly, and both defer to individual experience and gut comfort. The meta-analysis (Sun 2026) states that strategy-specific evidence is insufficient and that regained mass is not equivalent to rehydration. The numeric figures in the consensus are expert recommendations, not trial results in combat athletes. FightLab does not prescribe a cut or a recovery plan, so these figures belong with a sports dietitian, not in app instructions.
**Sources:** [F010], [F024], [F015]

### Do not promote water loading, diuretics or IV fluids
**Rule:** The app should not describe water loading, diuretics, laxatives, vomiting or IV fluids as options. If an athlete mentions them, it may say that diuretics are prohibited in most sports and that IV rehydration is prohibited by sport governing bodies, that evidence on water loading is limited and early, and that they should speak to a physician or sports dietitian.
**Evidence:** The ACSM statement lists diuretics as banned in most sports, with risks of electrolyte imbalance and muscle cramps. It describes water loading as a practice whose health and performance implications are only now emerging, with a small risk of hyponatraemia, and says surveys find low use of vomiting, banned diuretics and diet pills. Barley 2019 says one study suggests water loading of about 3% of body mass was safe and effective in grapplers without impairing performance, but more research is needed in striking and mixed styles and for losses above 4%; it also notes that laxatives, diet pills, diuretics, enemas and vomiting appear in practice. A systematic review of doping in combat sports (6 of 21 articles; Damjanovic 2025) reports diuretics among the substances commonly detected, especially in heavier categories and in athletes engaging in rapid weight loss. In the Barley 2018 survey, 67% of MMA respondents reported water loading.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Surveys and small trials in combat athletes, mostly men; doping review of 6 studies.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Water loading evidence is limited and partly favourable (Barley 2019 cites one study supporting about 3% in grapplers), so the app must not call it safe or unsafe: it says evidence is limited and refers. The doping review is small, relies partly on self-report and is not specifically about weight cutting.
**Sources:** [F010], [F024], [F068], [F013]

### Weigh-in rules are not a safety guarantee
**Rule:** The app may state that organisations use measures such as earlier or later weigh-ins, re-weigh limits, hydration (urine specific gravity) tests, minimum weight rules and extra weight classes, and that evidence on their effect is limited and mixed. The app should tell athletes to check the rules of their own event and commission, and should not suggest that passing a hydration test or a weigh-in means the athlete is safe.
**Evidence:** The ACSM statement describes the US collegiate wrestling programme introduced after the 1997 deaths. It limited loss to 1.5% of body mass per week, set a minimum weight at 5% body fat, moved the weigh-in to 1-2 hours before competition, added 3.2 kg to class limits, banned unsafe methods, randomised match order and required a urine specific gravity of 1.020 at weigh-in. Studies after the programme showed less seasonal body mass variation, better retention of lean mass and a fall in regain to 1.2 plus or minus 0.9 kg. The statement adds four caveats: the hydration test can misclassify euhydrated athletes as dehydrated; the earlier Wisconsin programme produced only a small reduction in harmful practices; judo and taekwondo now weigh in the evening before, with a morning re-weigh of a random sample that excludes athletes more than 5% above the class limit, whose effect needs evaluation and which athletes can manage their weight to; and there were no impact data for the California commission 10-point plan or the ONE Championship rules. A brief review (Loenneke 2011) notes that few studies have tested the NCAA minimum-weight methods in collegiate wrestlers and points out possible problems with the protocol. A retrospective cohort of North American professional MMA weigh-ins around the early weigh-in policy (Curran-Sills 2022) found that the share of athletes missing weight rose from 5.7% to 8.4% after the policy, and the mean excess rose from 1.3 kg to 1.8 kg; the authors concluded that the policy had not improved weight-cutting culture. A systematic review of the most cited literature (Karnincic 2024, 13 articles) says there has been no progress in regulating RWL in wrestling in 35 years. The 2026 meta-analysis says shorter windows may reduce the incentive for extreme cuts but may leave athletes inadequately hydrated unless they come with minimum-weight certification, education and hydration monitoring.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** US high-school and college wrestling (the NCAA programme), judo, taekwondo and professional MMA commissions. The Curran-Sills cohort covers professional MMA events in North America over 2014-2018 (total athlete count not in the abstract). The NCAA outcome studies are those the consensus cites and were not opened individually.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The NCAA programme seems to reduce regain and seasonal swings, but it bundles several measures and was not randomised. The MMA early weigh-in policy coincided with more missed weights, which is a different outcome from health. Morning re-weigh rules may be gamed. The 2026 meta-analysis argues for addressing the size of the cut and not recovery time alone.
**Sources:** [F010], [F069], [F067], [F015], [F070]

### No weight-cut content for minors, and refer repeated cutters
**Rule:** FightLab convention (age gate, supported by consensus): do not show weight-cut information to users under 18; instead say that sports-medicine consensus discourages weight making in young athletes, and refer them to a physician or sports dietitian. For adults, the app may explain that repeated cutting and prolonged low energy intake are linked in sports-medicine consensus to relative energy deficiency in sport (REDs), hormonal changes and, in some groups, disordered eating, and it should refer them to a sports physician or dietitian. FightLab convention (not a validated screen): the referral triggers are repeated cuts across a season, prolonged restriction, frequent weight cycling, persistent low energy, low mood, low libido, or a troubled relationship with food.
**Evidence:** The ACSM statement recommends that strategies discourage any weight making among minors and the youngest participants and fully inform adult competitors of the risks. A systematic review of youth Olympic combat sports (Lakicevic 2022) found RWL prevalence of 25-94% depending on sport, age and level, with losses from about 1% to 6.3 plus or minus 3.7% of body mass, using methods such as sauna and impermeable suits; its authors concluded that RWL should be strictly regulated and ideally banned in this age group. The ACSM statement reports falls in testosterone and IGF-1 during a three-to-four-month wrestling season, loss of fat-free mass across a season, and higher eating disorder questionnaire scores in some (not all) weight-category cohorts, and it places chronic low energy availability in weight-category athletes within the RED-S syndrome. The IOC 2023 REDs consensus (abstract) describes REDs as affecting female and male athletes exposed to low energy availability, notes new data on low carbohydrate availability, mental health and males, and introduces a clinical assessment tool with risk-based training and competition recommendations; the abstract does not address weight cutting specifically.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Youth and adult combat and weight-category athletes; wrestlers mostly male; the IOC consensus covers both sexes. The age gate and the trigger list are FightLab conventions.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Evidence on REDs in male combat athletes is limited: the IOC methodology paper notes that much REDs physiology is based on or extrapolated from limited studies, historically mostly in young female athletes. Bone density was not clearly harmed in the combat-athlete studies the ACSM statement cites. Eating disorder scores were higher in some cohorts but not all. The 18-year cut-off and the trigger list are FightLab conventions, not from the sources.
**Sources:** [F010], [F019], [F020], [F071]

### Collapse or confusion during a cut is an emergency
**Rule:** The app may state, as general information and not a diagnosis, that collapse, or signs that the brain is affected, during or after exercise or heat exposure can mean exertional heat stroke, which is a medical emergency: call emergency services, and rapid cooling saves lives; it is never something to push through. FightLab convention examples of brain signs: confusion, loss of coordination, unusual behaviour. FightLab convention for the further stop-and-seek-care list (for clinician review): chest pain or irregular heartbeat, severe dizziness or fainting on standing, persistent vomiting, severe muscle cramps, very dark or scant urine, and not being able to keep fluids down. For any of these, the app should tell the athlete to stop heat or sweat-based methods and seek medical care or call emergency services, and should not suggest any workaround.
**Evidence:** The ACSM position stand on exertional heat illness (Armstrong 2007, abstract) defines exertional heatstroke as rectal temperature above 40 C with signs of organ system failure, most often central nervous system dysfunction. It says heatstroke can strike seemingly healthy athletes even when conditions are relatively cool, that early recognition and rapid cooling reduce illness and death, and that the signs can be subtle and easy to miss. It names dehydration among the factors that make collapse from exhaustion in the heat more likely. It describes heat exhaustion as collapse from exhaustion in the heat, notes that on site only rectal temperature may tell severe heat exhaustion from heatstroke, and says exercise-associated muscle cramping usually responds to rest and fluid and salt replacement. The ACSM weight-making statement (Burke 2021) links deaths during weight cutting to cardiovascular complications and/or heat stroke, and mentions electrolyte disturbance, muscle cramps and acute renal stress as risks.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Exertional heat illness in athletes generally (position stand); weight-cutting deaths in wrestling and combat sports (case-based). The specific symptom examples and the further list are not in the opened sources.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The sources support the principle (collapse or central nervous system dysfunction during exertion in the heat is an emergency; early cooling) but give no validated symptom checklist for weight cutters, and the position stand addresses exertional, not passive, heat exposure. Collapse may be heat exhaustion rather than heatstroke, and only a core temperature reading can tell them apart, which the app cannot measure. The app must not imply it can detect heat stroke or dehydration. Severe cramps are usually benign according to the position stand; their inclusion in the stop list is a conservative FightLab convention.
**Sources:** [F018], [F010]

## Concussion and head impacts

### Stop training after a suspected head injury
**Rule:** If an athlete reports a blow to the head (or a hard blow to the body that jolts the head) with any concussion symptom, or a partner or coach sees warning signs (for example the athlete lies still, is slow to get up or unsteady, looks dazed or confused, loses consciousness, has a seizure or cannot remember what happened), the app should tell the athlete to stop training now. It should say not to return that day, and not to do sparring or any activity with a risk of head contact, falls or collision until a doctor or other qualified clinician has assessed them, even if the symptoms ease. The app must not tell the athlete whether they do or do not have a concussion.
**Evidence:** The Amsterdam 2022 consensus (6th International Conference on Concussion in Sport, written for healthcare professionals, informed by 10 systematic reviews) says a player should be removed on suspicion of concussion, based on symptoms or on signs seen by others. Signs that call for immediate removal include actual or suspected loss of consciousness, seizure, tonic posturing, poor balance or ataxia, confusion, behaviour change and amnesia; players with them should not return to a match or training that day unless an experienced clinician, after a multimodal assessment, decides the sign was not due to concussion. Symptoms and signs can evolve over minutes to days, so serial re-evaluation is advised. The Concussion Recognition Tool 6 (CRT6), meant for non-medically trained people and explicitly not a diagnostic tool, says an athlete with suspected concussion is removed at once and does not return to any activity with risk of head contact, fall or collision, including sport, until medically assessed, even if symptoms resolve. The consensus also reports that laws and protocols such as mandatory removal, required clinician clearance and education are associated with fewer recurrent concussions, and that its return-to-sport review linked continuing to play and late access to a clinician with longer recovery.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Consensus statement (31 authors) and recognition tool for athletes of all ages and levels, recreational to professional; the evidence base is mostly field and ice sports, not combat sports. The underlying data are mainly observational; the recommendation is expert consensus.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No conflicting guidance found. Evidence for same-day removal is observational and expert-based, but the recommendation is universal across international bodies. Prior concussions and individual factors change risk and recovery.
**Sources:** [F025], [F026], [F028]

### Red flags after a head blow mean emergency care
**Rule:** If, after a blow to the head or body, the athlete reports or others see a red-flag sign (for example neck pain, a seizure, losing consciousness, vision problems, worsening confusion or headache, or repeated vomiting), the app should replace coaching content with an urgent message to get emergency medical care now (call the local emergency number). It should also say that the athlete should not be left alone for at least the first 3 hours, should not be sent home alone, should avoid alcohol, recreational drugs and drugs not prescribed by their clinician, and should not drive until a health professional clears it. For the complete red-flag list the app should link to the official CRT6 rather than reproduce, reformat or re-typeset it, unless BMJ permission is obtained, and the final wording should be checked by a clinician.
**Evidence:** The official CRT6 (Concussion in Sport Group, BJSM 2023) lists ten red flags, signs of possible serious head or neck injury such as neck pain, seizures and a deteriorating conscious state, under an instruction to call an ambulance. If any is present after an impact to the head or body, the athlete is removed at once and taken for urgent care by a healthcare professional. The tool adds first-aid points (assume a possible spinal injury; do not move the athlete unless trained) and says an athlete with suspected concussion should not be left alone for at least the first 3 hours, should not go home alone, should not drink alcohol or take recreational or non-prescribed drugs, and should not drive until cleared. The consensus statement text itself has no red-flag list; it lives in the CRT6.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Recognition tool for children, adolescents and adults in all sports, developed by the Concussion in Sport Group as an output of the Amsterdam consensus; not combat-sport specific but applies to any blow to the head. Read from the official BJSM tool.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** None found in content. The list is expert consensus, not a validated decision rule. Modified reproductions differ (the FIS skiing version uses 1 to 2 hours and a shorter list), so only the official tool should be used. The CRT6 may be copied only unaltered; digital re-formatting or translation needs BMJ consent, which constrains how the app displays it.
**Sources:** [F026]

### Early rest after a concussion is relative
**Rule:** After a suspected concussion, the app should not tell the athlete to stay in a dark room or do nothing. It can say that relative rest (everyday activities, less screen time) applies for roughly the first 1 to 2 days and that light activity such as walking is generally acceptable if it does not make symptoms clearly worse. It should add that anything with a risk of contact, collision or falling (sparring, grappling, clinch, rolling, pad work with a partner) is off until a clinician says it is safe. If symptoms get clearly worse with activity, stop and rest until they return to the earlier level.
**Evidence:** The Amsterdam 2022 statement says strict rest until all symptoms resolve is not beneficial. Relative rest, including daily activities and reduced screen time, is indicated right after injury and for up to the first 2 days. Light physical activity such as walking that does not more than mildly worsen symptoms is allowed in the first 24 to 48 hours, and clinicians are encouraged to advise early return to physical activity as tolerated (for example walking or stationary cycling while avoiding contact, collision or falls) after 24 to 48 hours. Reduced screen use is warranted for the first 48 hours but may not help after that. Mild symptom exacerbation means a rise of no more than 2 points on a 0 to 10 scale lasting less than 1 hour; if exacerbation is more than mild and brief, stop the activity and resume once symptoms return to the earlier level. Athletes should avoid the risk of re-injury (contact, collision or fall) until a qualified clinician judges higher-risk activity to be safe. The statement also cites sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise prescribed by a clinician within 2 to 10 days as effective at reducing persisting symptoms; that is a clinician-supervised treatment and not something the app should prescribe.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** Mainly adolescent and adult athletes in mixed sports; the guidance sits on a systematic review and meta-analysis of rest and exercise that was cited but not opened separately. No combat-sport sample.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Individual recovery varies. The statement notes that sleep disturbance in the first 10 days is associated with persisting symptoms. Where it differs from older advice (strict cocoon rest), the consensus is explicit that strict rest is not recommended.
**Sources:** [F025]

### Return to contact follows a graded clinician-supervised plan
**Rule:** The app should show the six-step return-to-sport structure as information (symptom-limited activity, light then moderate aerobic exercise, individual sport-specific work away from the team, non-contact drills, full-contact practice, return to competition) and say that each step typically takes at least 24 hours, so a full return takes at least about a week and is typically completed within about a month. It must not compute a return date for the athlete and must not move the athlete between steps itself. Progression through the later steps is monitored and authorised by a clinician. If symptoms are not steadily improving 2 to 4 weeks after the injury, the app should tell the athlete to ask a clinician for a fuller evaluation.
**Evidence:** The 6th consensus statement's return-to-sport strategy has six steps: (1) symptom-limited daily activity, (2) aerobic exercise (2A light, up to about 55 percent of age-predicted maximum heart rate; 2B moderate, up to about 70 percent), with light resistance work only if it does not worsen symptoms more than mildly and briefly, (3) individual sport-specific exercise away from the team with no head-impact risk, (4) non-contact training drills at high intensity, (5) full-contact practice, (6) return to sport. Step 1 may start within 24 hours of injury and each later step typically takes at least 24 hours; steps 4 to 6 should be monitored by a healthcare professional. More than mild worsening in steps 1 to 3 means stop and try again the next day; symptoms in steps 4 to 6 mean going back to step 3. The statement says to expect at least 1 week for the full strategy and that typical unrestricted return can take up to 1 month. The return-to-sport systematic review and meta-analysis behind it found a pooled mean time to return of 19.8 days (95 percent CI 18.8 to 20.7, 57 studies, I-squared 99.3 percent); that review included 278 studies, about four in five cohort studies, mostly North American, with under 1 in 10 rated high quality. Symptoms that persist, worsen or do not steadily resolve 2 to 4 weeks after injury call for a multimodal evaluation and rehabilitation referral; symptoms beyond 4 weeks are termed persisting symptoms. Time to unrestricted return has risen about fivefold since these strategies were introduced, and recovery varies with factors such as migraine history, anxiety and post-injury stress.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** General sport-related concussion in children, adolescents and adults; pooled recovery times come from mixed, mostly North American field and ice sport cohorts; no combat-sport sample. The 55 and 70 percent heart-rate figures use 220 minus age, which is crude. The strong grade rests on the consensus strategy; the 19.8-day figure is descriptive and comes from one meta-analysis with very high heterogeneity.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Recovery time varies widely (I-squared 99.3 percent). The statement says time frames must be individualised; 19.8 days is a pooled average, not a target. Federation protocols differ in initial rest and total time (see "Do not present headgear as concussion protection").
**Sources:** [F025], [F028]

### No sparring until symptom-free with exertion and cleared
**Rule:** After a reported head blow with symptoms, the app should keep the no_contact flag on and block planned sparring, clinch work, grappling with live resistance, partner drilling and any other activity with head-impact, collision or fall risk. Wording should say that clearance is a medical decision, that symptoms must be gone both at rest and during and after hard effort, and that full-contact practice is the second-to-last stage of the return, after non-contact work and before competition. FightLab convention: the flag is lifted only after the athlete confirms in the app that a clinician has cleared a return to contact; the app never lifts it on a timer and never suggests the athlete is probably fine. FightLab convention: when showing the stages, solo technical work counts as step 3, cooperative partner drilling without head contact as step 4 and sparring or live rolling as step 5, and any partner work waits for clinician clearance.
**Evidence:** The Amsterdam statement says steps 4 to 6 begin only after symptoms, cognitive abnormalities and other clinical findings from the injury have resolved, including with and after physical exertion, and only after a clinician authorises it. Step 3 also needs medical clearance first if sport-specific training carries any risk of inadvertent head impact. A clinician's written determination of readiness should come before unrestricted return, as local laws or sport rules require, and medical determination of readiness should come before any activity with contact, collision or fall risk. The statement defines symptom resolution at rest, complete symptom resolution (no symptoms during or after maximal physical and cognitive exertion) and return to sport (strategy completed with no symptoms or clinical findings at rest and with maximal exertion). The CRT6 says no return to activity with risk of head contact, fall or collision until medically assessed, even if symptoms resolve. A 2026 judo adaptation likewise reintroduces partner drills and randori only after clinical recovery, with medical clearance before full-contact training. The statement uses team-sport examples; the mapping of combat-sport activities to steps is FightLab's, not the consensus's.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** General sport-related concussion at all levels; applies to combat sports by extension, not from combat-sport data. The strong grade covers only the consensus requirements (symptom resolution with exertion, clinician authorisation before at-risk steps). The flag mechanics and the step mapping are FightLab conventions and carry no evidence grade.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** None found in the consensus. Self-reported clearance cannot be verified by the app. Local laws and sanctioning bodies may add their own rules (for example written clearance or minimum stand-down periods).
**Sources:** [F025], [F026], [F072]

### Concussion is common in combat-sport competition
**Rule:** The app can state that concussion is among the most common serious injuries in MMA and boxing competition, that rates vary a lot between studies, and that the best data are from competition rather than training. It should not quote a single injury rate as a personal risk.
**Evidence:** Lystad et al. 2014 pooled 6 MMA competition studies: 228.7 injuries per 1000 athlete-exposures (95 percent CI 110.4 to 473.5); the head was the most injured region (66.8 to 78.0 percent of injuries) and concussion made up 3.8 to 20.4 percent of injuries. Losers had about 3 times as many injuries as winners, and knockout or technical-knockout bouts more than twice as many as submission bouts. A 2025 systematic review of 43 MMA reports after the 2009 Unified Rules found concussion rates of 14.7 to 16.1 per 100 athlete-exposures, with varied definitions and little training or female data. A 2026 MMA meta-analysis (search to July 2025) reported a pooled concussion prevalence of 20.68 percent (95 percent CI 17.66 to 23.69); its abstract does not say whether athletes or injuries are the denominator and its report counts are inconsistent, so this figure is weak. For boxing, a 2023 meta-analysis of 14 studies from this century found concussion was 12.3 percent (95 percent CI 8.7 to 15.9) of injuries, with higher injury rates in professional than amateur boxing. A 2023 meta-analysis of head trauma in boxing (35 studies) reported a higher concussion risk for boxers than other combat sports (risk ratios 0.253 versus 0.065). In contrast, a Turkish series of 663 Muay Thai athletes at official bouts found that 1.50 percent had a concussion, about 4 percent head or extremity injuries and a referee stoppage in 24.58 percent of contests, which the authors call low. No systematic review of kickboxing or Muay Thai concussion incidence was found.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Mostly male professional and amateur competition (the Muay Thai series was about one third women); MMA reviews rest on few studies, boxing reviews on 14 to 35 studies; definitions of injury and concussion and exposure units differ. Muay Thai data are from one national ringside series. Competition, not sparring.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Concussion shares (3.8 to 20.4 percent in MMA; 12.3 percent in boxing; 1.5 percent of athletes in one Muay Thai series) differ with definitions, level and rules. The boxing head-trauma meta-analysis reports its risk ratios without a clear comparison, so only its direction is used. The 2026 MMA meta-analysis has an unclear denominator. Training injuries are rarely reported, so these rates cannot be applied to sparring.
**Sources:** [F029], [F030], [F073], [F031], [F074], [F075]

### Head injuries also happen in training
**Rule:** The app can say that most documented head injuries come from competition, and that concussion still occurs in practice and sparring, so a head blow in the gym should be handled with the same stop-and-refer rule as a head blow in competition.
**Evidence:** A 2022 meta-analysis of 17 amateur boxing cohort studies found a competition injury rate of 54.7 per 1000 athlete-exposures (95 percent CI 33.8 to 88.4) and a training injury rate of 1.3 per 1000 hours (95 percent CI 0.2 to 7.0), about one injury every 2.5 hours of competition and every 772 hours of training. Head and neck injuries predominated in competition (median 72 percent), while in training the upper limb was the most common region and sprains and strains the most common type; the training figure covers all injuries, not concussion alone. A US emergency-department surveillance study of martial-arts concussions over 2013 to 2022 (581 cases, 29.1 percent female) found that falls (23 percent) and being punched, hit or struck (20 percent) were the commonest documented mechanisms, and that among males judo and karate had more concussions in class or practice than other disciplines; 96.4 percent were treated and released and 3.1 percent admitted.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Amateur boxing (pooled cohorts); US emergency-department cases across karate, jiu-jitsu, taekwondo, MMA, kickboxing, judo and other forms (ages not given in the abstract read); counts include only cases that reached an emergency department, with no exposure denominator.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The training injury rate has a very wide confidence interval and few training studies exist. Emergency-department data cannot give rates. Whether hard sparring has a higher concussion rate than technical work is not answered by these data.
**Sources:** [F076], [F077]

### Sparring delivers many head impacts of variable size
**Rule:** The app can tell athletes that sparring exposes the head to repeated impacts, that the number and size vary a lot between sessions and people, and that sensors in the studies are research tools with known accuracy problems. It should not claim to estimate head-impact dose, and it should not use session RPE or round counts as a measure of head-impact load without saying that this is a FightLab convention and not a validated measure.
**Evidence:** Instrumented-mouthguard and headgear studies in sparring: Jansen et al. (23 amateur boxing and MMA athletes, 4 women, 53 training sessions of 5 to 20 minutes and 6 competitions) verified 896 impacts, 827 in practice; impacts per session were similar in boxing and MMA, MMA impacts had higher peak angular acceleration, and men had more impacts per session than women with no sex difference in magnitude. Doan et al. (27 male collegiate boxers, two 2-minute sparring rounds) recorded on average 27.6 impacts above a 9.6 g threshold per boxer, mean peak linear acceleration 23.5 g, lower magnitude than reported for amateur boxing. Fetchko et al. (22 male military cadets, beginner boxing course, 205 full-contact bouts) video-verified 809 impacts, about 3.9 per bout, mean peak linear acceleration 16.5 g (maximum 70.8 g), 79 percent between 10 and 20 g, similar to service-academy football. Tiernan et al. (13 MMA fighters) verified 451 impacts above 10 g over 19 sparring and 11 competition events; all 5 doctor-diagnosed concussions occurred in competition, and concussive events had 24.7 percent higher maximum angular acceleration and 189 percent higher head impact power. A single elite Muay Thai case recorded 590 device-flagged impacts in 7 sparring sessions; most were low linear loads but some angular peaks reached 19,862 to 26,850 rad/s2, unverified by video, so the authors urge caution. Sensor quality is a problem: in boxing sparring only 53 percent (mouthguard), 26 percent (headgear patch) and 20 percent (skin patch) of 442 events per sensor had high-quality traces, and low-quality traces overstated magnitude. No study compared hard sparring with technical or light sparring directly.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Small convenience samples; men in the studies that report sex (plus 4 women in one study), sex not reported in the abstracts of three studies; amateur, collegiate, military cadet, professional or semi-professional MMA and one elite Muay Thai athlete; different sensors and thresholds make studies not comparable.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Different sensors, thresholds and verification methods give different counts and magnitudes. The Muay Thai peaks are unverified. Peak linear acceleration alone does not define concussion risk. Sensor data quality limits comparisons.
**Sources:** [F078], [F079], [F080], [F081], [F082], [F083]

### Feeling normal after sparring does not rule out head injury
**Rule:** The app can say that small, short-lived changes in memory or motor function have been measured after sparring bouts in research settings, and that a normal-feeling session does not rule out a head injury. A post-sparring symptom check inside the app should be limited to prompting the athlete to report symptoms and follow the stop-and-refer rule in "Stop training after a suspected head injury", not to scoring or reassuring.
**Evidence:** Di Virgilio et al. (20 amateur boxers, three 3-minute sparring bouts, 20 controls doing mock sparring) found increased corticomotor inhibition, altered motor-unit recruitment and lower memory performance 1 hour after sparring compared with controls, back to baseline by 24 hours. Doan et al. found small declines in delayed memory and small improvements in response time from before to after bouts in 27 collegiate boxers, with number of impacts and prior concussion predicting worse memory. In contrast, Le Flao et al. (7 competitive boxers, symptom scale before, right after and 48 hours after sparring, instrumented mouthguards) found no group change in symptom severity from before to after sparring (median change -1, p 0.85) and lower symptom scores at 48 hours than before; 4 of 16 sessions, from 3 boxers, showed a rise of more than 5 points, and the link to impact exposure was unclear. The authors favour individual-specific approaches.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Amateur, collegiate and competitive boxers; very small samples (7, 20, 27); sex reported only in the collegiate study (men). One study had control participants.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Cognitive and motor tests show small acute effects, while a symptom scale in a different small sample did not. Findings cannot be used to set a safe dose of head contact.
**Sources:** [F084], [F079], [F085]

### Cumulative fight exposure is linked to brain changes
**Rule:** The app can state that studies of professional fighters link greater cumulative exposure with lower brain volumes in some regions and slower processing speed, that these studies are observational and cannot show cause, and that results for sparring specifically are mixed. It should encourage fighters to reduce avoidable head contact in training as a FightLab convention, not as a proven dose-response rule, and should avoid telling any individual that their brain is or is not damaged.
**Evidence:** In the Professional Fighters Brain Health Study (cross-sectional baseline analysis of 224 licensed professional fighters, 131 MMA and 93 boxers, and 22 controls), more professional fights, more years of fighting and a higher fight exposure score were associated with lower volumes in several brain regions, particularly thalamus and caudate, and slower processing speed; higher exposure scores went with a greater chance of cognitive impairment. In the longitudinal analysis (204 participants with at least 2 assessments), active boxers lost left thalamus volume about 102.3 mm3 per year faster than controls and also lost corpus callosum volume, retired boxers lost amygdala and hippocampus volume faster than controls, and higher baseline blood neurofilament light was linked to greater loss in some regions. In the plasma study (471 participants), the number of sparring rounds by active fighters correlated with neurofilament light but not tau. In contrast, in 94 active professional MMA fighters (cross-sectional), more weekly sparring rounds was not associated with smaller volumes in any of five regions and was associated with larger caudate volume (about 13.5 to 14.9 microlitres per weekly round), which the authors say could reflect training effects, baseline differences or other mechanisms. In 212 professional fighters, a better win-loss record in retired fighters went with smaller volumes in several regions, slower processing speed and more impulsiveness. The Amsterdam systematic review of cohort and case-control studies (10 studies of former amateur and 18 of former professional athletes, mostly American football and soccer) found no increased mental health or neurological disease risk in former amateurs; some studies of former professionals reported higher dementia or ALS mortality, but most had high risk of bias and did not control for confounders. The consensus says brain-bank studies of CTE neuropathologic change cannot establish causation or quantify risk, that extensive repetitive head impacts may plausibly be associated with it, and that it is a post-mortem finding, not a clinical diagnosis.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Licensed professional boxers and MMA fighters who volunteered for MRI and testing (sex not reported in the abstracts read; selection bias likely), with few controls (22 at baseline); the systematic review covers mostly American football and soccer, not combat sports.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Exposure-volume associations in fighters contrast with no association of sparring rounds with smaller volumes in MMA fighters (and a positive association with caudate), while sparring rounds did correlate with blood neurofilament light. The systematic review sees no signal in amateurs and an uncertain signal in professionals. Confounders (education, vascular risk, sleep, alcohol and other exposures) are not fully controlled. Do not present this as proof that sparring harms the brain or that a given number of rounds is safe.
**Sources:** [F086], [F087], [F088], [F089], [F090], [F091], [F025]

### Do not present headgear as concussion protection
**Rule:** The app should not say that headgear, mouthguards or light sparring prevent concussion in combat sports. It can say that headgear reduces cuts and probably ear injury but has not been shown to prevent concussion, and that concussion protocols in combat-sport organisations vary (as of a September 2023 comparison, none yet reflected the 2022 consensus), so athletes should follow the rules of their own federation and the advice of their own clinician.
**Evidence:** The Association of Ringside Physicians position statement (2024) says headguards are highly effective for reducing facial lacerations in amateur boxing and other striking sports and, on limited wrestling data, ear injury, but should not be relied on to reduce concussion or other brain injury: no protective effect has been shown, human studies are lacking, biomechanical studies show lower head accelerations, and more risk-taking in headguard users may offset any benefit. The Amsterdam consensus likewise says headgear in non-helmeted contact sports needs more research. A 2023 boxing meta-analysis found headguard use associated with more stoppages in amateur bouts (odds ratio 1.75 versus 0.53) and called the efficiency data limited. A comparison of the concussion rules of 12 international organisations from contact, collision and combat sports found only 6 had their own published protocols; all required at least one medical examination before clearance; initial rest, sport-specific drills and time to complete varied widely; and none had been updated to the latest consensus as of 9 September 2023, with the authors stressing combat sports. The Association of Ringside Physicians 2026 position on high-risk competitors lists concussion history, incomplete recovery or lack of clearance from the latest concussion as a high-risk criterion (evidence level II; three consecutive losses level III; other criteria level V). A 2026 judo adaptation of Amsterdam adds judo-specific stages and reintroduces partner drills and randori only after clinical recovery, with medical clearance before full-contact training; it needs prospective validation.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Position statements by ringside physicians for boxing, MMA and other combat sports (expert opinion on limited data); headguard studies mostly in amateur boxers; the judo protocol is an untested proposal; the federation comparison is a document review.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Mouthguards were associated with a 28 percent lower concussion rate in ice hockey in the consensus evidence, so the do-not-claim rule is limited to combat sports, where no such data were found. No peer-reviewed guidance was found that caps sparring head contact or defines return to sparring specifically for MMA, boxing, kickboxing or Muay Thai. The headguard stoppage result is hard to interpret (stoppages are not concussions). Federation protocols may have been updated since September 2023.
**Sources:** [F032], [F025], [F074], [F027], [F092], [F072]

### A head blow with symptoms sets no-contact and refers
**Rule:** Suggested app behaviour. When the athlete logs a blow to the head with any symptom (headache, dizziness, nausea, blurred vision, balance problem, feeling off, confusion, memory gap, fatigue, irritability), or reports signs seen by others, the app should (1) show the emergency message first if any red flag applies (see "Red flags after a head blow mean emergency care"); (2) tell the athlete to stop training now and do no more contact work that day; (3) say they should be assessed by a doctor or other qualified clinician, who decides whether it is a concussion; (4) FightLab convention: set no_contact and remove sparring, clinch, partner drilling and live grappling from the plan until the athlete confirms clinician clearance; (5) say that everyday activity and light exercise such as walking are fine from the start if they do not clearly worsen symptoms; and (6) never state a diagnosis, a probability of concussion, a number of days or a safe-to-spar message. Suggested wording (FightLab text, to be checked by a clinician): "A blow to the head with symptoms needs to be checked by a doctor. Stop training for today and do not spar or do any contact work until a clinician has cleared you. If you have a warning sign such as neck pain, a seizure, passing out, worsening confusion or headache, or repeated vomiting, get emergency care now. The full list of warning signs is in the official Concussion Recognition Tool 6." FightLab convention: the trigger symptom list, and using a repeated or recent head blow, a prior concussion or an upcoming fight date to make the messaging more cautious, are product choices; none may shorten or relax the rule.
**Evidence:** This rule combines the opened sources: removal on suspicion and no return to contact until medically assessed, red flags, relative rest with light activity allowed and contact avoided until a clinician judges it safe, and the graded return with clinician authorisation before any at-risk step (see the rules above). The CRT6 is designed for non-medically trained people to identify suspected concussion and states that it does not diagnose. The SCAT6 and SCOAT6 are clinician tools (SCAT6 most useful within 72 hours and up to 1 week, SCOAT6 from 72 hours onward), the consensus says screening short of a multimodal clinical evaluation may be inadequate to allow continued play, and computerised cognitive tests should not be used in isolation for management or diagnosis. These support not building a self-scored concussion test into the app. Federation protocols reviewed in 2023 all required at least one medical examination before clearance. Symptoms can evolve over minutes to days, supporting a prompt to report later-onset symptoms.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** General sport-related concussion guidance applied to combat-sport athletes of all levels. The strong grade is justified only by the consensus elements (remove on suspicion, no contact until medically assessed, refer, clinician-authorised graded return); the no_contact flag mechanics, the trigger list and the tone modifiers are FightLab conventions without their own evidence grade. The rule itself has not been tested in combat sports.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Whether to also flag a head blow with no symptoms is a product decision; the consensus supports removal on suspicion and serial reassessment, not a separate rule for symptom-free blows. A conservative flag may annoy athletes who feel fine; the CRT6 and the consensus both say to withhold return until medically assessed even if symptoms resolve. The app cannot verify a self-reported clearance.
**Sources:** [F025], [F026], [F027], [F072]

## Intensity of combat training

### Hard striking sparring is vigorous in small samples
**Rule:** FightLab can class hard or match-pace sparring in striking sports (boxing, Muay Thai, kickboxing) and MMA sparring as vigorous, and should present this as vigorous in the small samples measured, not as a universal fact. Light, technical or long-pause sparring will sit lower, and intensity changes with pace, opponent, level and round format.
**Evidence:** In nine experienced male amateur boxers, sparring done in the lab averaged about 43 ml/kg/min, around 70% of VO2peak and 85.5% HRmax, with lactate about 6.1 mmol/L; the same sparring in the gym gave 91.7% HRmax and 9.4 mmol/L. Both HR values sit in the vigorous band of the base rule "ACSM intensity bands" (cardio-health), 77-95% HRmax. In ten men during a simulated Muay Thai match, oxygen uptake and HR stayed above each athlete's anaerobic threshold for the whole match. In thirteen MMA athletes over three 5-min sparring rounds, blood lactate rose from about 4 to 15.6 mmol/L (the abstract prints the unit as mmol.dl, evidently a typo for mmol/L), with an effort to pause ratio near 1:3. In karate randori (free sparring), lactate after the session was 13.2 mmol/L and CR10 was 5.3, although the session-mean HR (73.9% HRmax) sat in the moderate band (see "Technical drilling runs below sparring"). In kickboxing small combat games, facing two or four rotating partners within a round raised HR, %HRpeak and RPE compared with one partner, while lactate did not change.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Single small observational studies (n = 9 to 20) of young adult competitors, amateur to national or international level. The boxing (n = 9) and Muay Thai (n = 10) samples were men; the MMA (n = 13, mean 25 years) and kickboxing (n = 20, mean 20 years) abstracts do not report sex; the karate sample was 8 women and 10 men, all black belts. No recreational or older athletes.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The MMA study's authors describe their sparring as moderate to high intensity, not uniformly vigorous. Session-mean HR can fall in the moderate band while lactate and RPE are high. Lab sparring was lower than gym sparring in the boxing study. Judo randori is reported to be less demanding than real matches. The kickboxing study gives no absolute HR values, so it shows that format moves intensity but not which band it reaches.
**Sources:** [F033], [F093], [F094], [F034], [F095]

### Class pad and bag work by pace
**Rule:** Do not give pad work or bag work one fixed class. FightLab should class it by pace and effort: hard-pace pad rounds and fast bag rounds as vigorous, slow technical bag rounds as moderate. Any default used when pace is unknown must be labelled a FightLab convention, and the athlete's RPE should override it.
**Evidence:** In nine male amateur boxers, pad work cost about 41 ml/kg/min (66% of VO2peak, 83.6% HRmax), and VO2 did not differ from sparring. Bag work at a set pace of 60, 120 and 180 punches per minute cost about 25, 30 and 38 ml/kg/min (40%, 49% and 62% of VO2peak) and 67.5%, 74.8% and 83.0% HRmax. VO2 at 60 and 120 per minute was lower than the other exercises; at 180 per minute it was not. Against the base rule "ACSM intensity bands" (cardio-health), pad work and the 180 per minute bag pace fall in the vigorous HR band (77-95% HRmax), while the 60 and 120 per minute paces fall in the moderate band (64-76% HRmax).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Nine experienced male amateur boxers of regional and provincial level (mean age 22, mean 13 bouts); one laboratory study.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Only boxing was measured; kicking, Muay Thai and MMA pad or bag work were not found. Metronome-paced bag work in a lab may differ from free bag rounds in a gym. The abstract reports statistics for VO2 only and describes HR as following a similar pattern.
**Sources:** [F033]

### Technical drilling runs below sparring
**Rule:** Technical or tactical drill sessions should not be classed the same as sparring. FightLab can treat technical drilling as lower than sparring (light to moderate by HR) and sparring as vigorous, labelling exact thresholds, including any CR10 cut-off between classes, as FightLab conventions, since the base rule "ACSM intensity bands" (cardio-health) gives RPE bands on the 6-20 scale only. The app should expect session-mean HR to understate how hard a sparring session was.
**Evidence:** In 18 black-belt karatekas over 65-75 min sessions (each with a 15 min warm-up and passive rests), the session-mean HR was 62.8% HRmax in technical-development drills, 67.8% in tactical-technical sparring situations and 73.9% in randori (free sparring). Lactate 3 min after the session was 6.6, 9.7 and 13.2 mmol/L, and CR10 RPE 30 min after was 2.9, 3.9 and 5.3, in the same order; every modality differed from the others on all three measures. On the base rule "ACSM intensity bands" (cardio-health), the drill-session mean sits in the light band (57-63% HRmax), the tactical-technical mean in the moderate band (64-76%) and the randori mean in the upper moderate band, even though randori lactate was very high. Women showed higher %HRmax than men across modalities (about 70 versus 66%), and international athletes reported lower lactate and RPE than national ones.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** 18 karatekas, mean age about 23 (9 national and 9 international level, each 4 women and 5 men), training at least seven times per week; one training camp shortly before a world championship. Karate is not one of FightLab's core sports, so transfer to boxing, Muay Thai, MMA or grappling is inferred.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** One study, one sport. Lactate after the technical-development session (6.6 mmol/L) was not low, so the light label rests on HR and RPE only. In randori, HR (moderate band) and lactate and RPE (hard) disagree. Session means include warm-up and rests, which pull mean HR down. The same session reads differently by sex (HR) and by level (lactate, RPE).
**Sources:** [F034]

### Class drills by structure, and judo randori sits below competition
**Rule:** For throwing, kicking and grappling drills, FightLab should classify by structure (all-out repetition blocks with short rest versus slow technical repetition) rather than by the label drill. Judo randori should not be assumed equal to competition. Because HR is reported to be an unreliable intensity measure in judo training modalities, the athlete's RPE should carry more weight than HR for these sessions; any default class is a FightLab convention.
**Evidence:** In ten judo athletes (mean 25 years, about 15 years of practice), all-out uchi-komi blocks raised oxygen uptake from about 32.5 ml/kg/min in the first minute to about 41.5 and 43.0 in the second and third, and the authors state that oxygen uptake and HR were similar to match simulations (no %HRmax or %VO2max values in the abstract). A narrative review of judo-specific training concludes that randori is the training mode closest to a match but less physiologically demanding than real competition, and that HR has not been an accurate intensity measure in uchi-komi, nage-komi or randori. In 18 adolescent male taekwondo athletes, technical kicking intervals of 10 s on and 20 s off produced the same %HR reserve and training load as interval running intended at 90-95% HRmax, and were rated hard to very hard.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Ten judo athletes (sex not reported in the abstract), a narrative review of judo studies, and 18 adolescent male taekwondo athletes. The adolescents are outside FightLab's adult population.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No opened source gives HR or lactate for slow technical repetition in judo; the only such data are the karate data in "Technical drilling runs below sparring". The uchi-komi abstract gives absolute VO2 only, so it cannot be placed on the base rule's %HRmax bands. The judo review's point that HR is unreliable weakens any HR-based class for these drills.
**Sources:** [F096], [F097], [F098]

### Grappling roll intensity class is a FightLab convention
**Rule:** FightLab should not claim an evidence-based intensity class for BJJ or wrestling live rolling. If it assigns one (for example vigorous for hard rolling, moderate for flow rolling), it must be labelled a FightLab convention, and the app should lean on the athlete's RPE.
**Evidence:** In seven male BJJ athletes, an all-out guard-passing test produced HR and lactate no different from a simulated BJJ combat, and RPE in the later test sets matched the combat; the abstract gives no values. In nine elite male BJJ athletes, lactate after a 10 min simulated combat was similar to bouts 3 and 4 of a specific anaerobic test and correlated with bouts 3 to 5 (r about 0.80 to 0.83), with no such link for HR or RPE. A systematic review of BJJ athletes (58 studies, 1496 participants) reports VO2max of 42-52 ml/kg/min and describes matches as intermittent high, moderate and low intensity actions; its abstract gives no HR or lactate for rolling. A short review found higher match RPE in MMA than in BJJ and kickboxing. A wrestling review abstract gives no match or training intensity figures.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Small samples of male BJJ athletes (n = 7 and 9, experienced or elite) plus a BJJ systematic review (1496 participants, which itself calls for studies in women) and narrative reviews; training rolls were not measured in any opened abstract.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Simulated competition differs from training rolls in pace and rest. No opened source gave %HRmax for rolling. The BJJ and judo reviews both stress the intermittent structure, which lowers mean HR relative to effort.
**Sources:** [F099], [F100], [F101], [F035], [F102]

### Do not class a combat session by mean heart rate alone
**Rule:** FightLab should allow RPE and HR to disagree, and should not downgrade a hard sparring session because the average HR looks moderate. When both are available, show both and let the athlete's RPE stand. Any CR10 value used to separate moderate from vigorous is a FightLab convention.
**Evidence:** Combat bouts are intermittent: in amateur boxing competitions activity-to-rest ratios of about 18:1 in elite and 9:1 in novice boxers are reported; in kickboxing combats about 1:1 in elite and 1:2 to 1:5 in amateur and national-level athletes; in simulated MMA sparring an effort to pause ratio of about 1:3. In karate randori, session-mean HR was 73.9% HRmax while lactate was 13.2 mmol/L. A short review of combat sports reports that RPE and session-RPE relate to lactate and HR, with session-RPE versus HR-based load correlations of 0.52 to 0.95, stronger in official competition than in training, and an RPE-lactate correlation of 0.81 in striking versus 0.53 in grappling matches. In 29 judo athletes across two mixed technical and randori sessions, mean and minimum HR were very stable between sessions (ICC 0.95 and 0.94), yet neither HR nor lactate was related to session-RPE; the authors still concluded that RPE is an appropriate workload measure across levels and sexes.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Judo (n = 29, both sexes, international training camp), karate (n = 18, both sexes), MMA sparring (n = 13), plus narrative reviews of boxing (male amateur competitions), kickboxing and mixed combat sports. The activity-to-rest ratios come from competition or simulated combat, not training sessions.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The review calls RPE valid in combat sports, yet the mixed-session judo study found no link between session-RPE and HR or lactate. Weaker RPE-HR correlations in training than in competition mean disagreement is expected in exactly the sessions FightLab logs. The reviews are short narrative reviews.
**Sources:** [F035], [F103], [F104], [F036], [F034], [F094]

## Session-RPE, monotony and the PMC

### Session-RPE is a valid general measure of internal load
**Rule:** FightLab may present minutes x CR10 RPE as a validated, practical estimate of internal session load for athletes in general: it was checked against heart-rate-based scores and reviews support its stand-alone use. It is a perceived-effort score in arbitrary units, not a physiological measurement, and the app should not compare absolute values between athletes.
**Evidence:** In the original method paper (Foster 2001), session-RPE and a heart-rate-based score moved together in steady-state and interval cycling and in basketball practice, the regression lines of the two study parts were almost the same, and session-RPE gave higher absolute scores than the HR method. As tabulated in Haddad 2017, the cycling part had 6 men and 6 women (about 23 y) and the basketball part 14 men (about 20 y). A mini review (Haddad 2017) found 950 papers citing Foster's method and 36 that tested its validity and reliability with the modified CR10 scale; it judged the method valid, reliable and internally consistent across several sports, both sexes, ages from children to adults and different levels, usable on its own, while noting that some authors pair it with heart rate. A meta-analysis of team-sport training and match data (McLaren 2018; 13 studies, 295 athletes, 10,418 sessions) found that session-RPE load correlated with total distance at r = 0.79 (90% CI 0.74 to 0.83), with accelerometer load at r = 0.63, with impacts at r = 0.57 and with high-speed distance at r = 0.47; correlations were highest in mixed training and smaller in other training modes (change in r from -0.08 to -0.58), so the link depends on training mode.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Foster 2001: cycling part 6 men and 6 women, basketball part 14 men (sample sizes as tabulated in Haddad 2017, not in the Foster abstract). Haddad 2017: mini review of 36 validation studies, mixed sports, ages and levels. McLaren 2018: team-sport athletes only (295 athletes in 13 studies); no combat sports were pooled.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Haddad 2017 is a mini review with a database search, not a quantitative synthesis; its main validity criterion is agreement with HR-based scores, which are themselves imperfect. McLaren 2018 used magnitude-based inference and pooled only team sports, so it supports session-RPE tracking external work in a mode-dependent way, not validity in combat sports. Session-RPE is influenced by non-load factors (see the next rules).
**Sources:** [F037], [F038], [F039]

### Session-RPE tracks heart-rate load in taekwondo and karate
**Rule:** For striking arts (taekwondo, karate, and by extension similar sports), the app can say that session-RPE has been compared with heart-rate-based load in small combat-sport samples and usually correlates moderately to strongly, but that agreement varies with session type, age and sport and was absent in one study of short high-intensity interval sessions. It should not claim that RPE and HR will agree in every session.
**Evidence:** A short review of RPE in combat sports (Slimani 2017) reports correlations between session-RPE and Edwards' HR-based load of r = 0.58 to 0.95 and with Banister's TRIMP of r = 0.52 to 0.86, with closer agreement in more aerobic combat (karate) than in more anaerobic combat (taekwondo), in adults than in young athletes, and in official competition than in training. In 10 young male taekwondo athletes (13.1 +/- 2.4 y) over 35 sessions (308 individual sessions), session-RPE correlated with HR-based loads at r = 0.55 to 0.90 across training modes (Haddad 2011). In 18 elite karate athletes (10 men, 8 women) over eight camp sessions, session-RPE correlated with two HR-based methods at r = 0.65 to 0.95, the same-intensity rating was consistent across sessions (Cronbach's alpha 0.81, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.92), and RPE related to percent maximal HR (r = 0.75) and blood lactate (r = 0.62) (Tabben 2015). In 9 prepubescent taekwondo athletes (100 sessions), session-RPE and Edwards' load correlated significantly in every session type and rating time, but the correlation was large (r = 0.71) only for pre-competitive sessions rated at 30 min (Lupo 2017). In contrast, in 18 adolescent male taekwondo athletes (14 +/- 2 y) doing short high-intensity interval sessions (10 s work, 20 s rest, running or taekwondo technique), session-RPE did not correlate significantly with Banister's TRIMP (r = 0.14) or Edwards' load (r = 0.20) (Haddad 2011b).
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Young or elite taekwondo and karate athletes, mostly male (Tabben 2015 and Lupo 2017 include women and girls); samples of 9 to 18 per study; camps or one-week to short monitoring periods. Slimani 2017 is a short narrative review across striking and grappling sports, novice to elite. No adult amateur or recreational fighters.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Agreement depends on session type and age: strong in aerobic or mixed sessions, absent in one study of short high-intensity intervals (Haddad 2011b), which the authors linked to the larger anaerobic contribution. All samples are small and young, the studies are observational, and several share the same research group. No meta-analysis of session-RPE validity in combat sports was opened.
**Sources:** [F035], [F105], [F098], [F106], [F107]

### Treat session-RPE as perceived load in grappling, MMA, boxing and judo
**Rule:** For MMA, BJJ, boxing, wrestling and judo, the app should present session-RPE load as a useful perceived-load record that tracks training changes and context, and avoid saying it has been validated against heart rate in these sports. Athletes should expect that how they feel (fatigue, soreness, sleep) can influence the rating.
**Evidence:** In 20 MMA athletes followed for two weeks (Kirk 2024), mean whole-session load was 448.6 AU (SD 191.1); Bayesian regressions between internal (RPE-based) and accelerometer loads explained R2 = 0.50 to 0.77 for most training categories but only 0.17 for the warm-up, 0.27 for BJJ drills, 0.49 for BJJ sparring and 0.13 for the session as a whole, so perceived and external movement load related weakly for whole sessions and grappling drills. In a sparring study (Kirk 2024b; 10 MMA athletes, 3 x 5 min bouts), athletes with higher VO2max stayed at sRPE 4 to 6 while those with lower VO2max rose above 7. In 12 BJJ athletes over 7 weeks (Campos 2022), the weeks with higher session-RPE load, monotony and strain came with higher stress scores and cortisol and lower salivary testosterone and IgA; the authors considered session-RPE reliable and simple, but no direct HR validation was reported. In 61 judo athletes aged 14 to 17 (Ouergui 2020), session-RPE correlated positively with sleep, fatigue, muscle soreness and the Hooper index during intensified training and was partly predicted by recovery state and fatigue. In 8 boxers (Uchida 2014), session-RPE rose with session intensity (about 1.3 to 1.7 easy, 2.5 to 2.7 moderate, 5.7 to 5.8 hard, in AU) together with heart-rate reserve and lactate. A mini review (Haddad 2017) notes that fatigue, stress, soreness and sleep may change RPE, but cites a study where they were not major contributors in a 10 min submaximal effort at non-excessive loads, and says the high-load case has not been tested.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** 20 MMA athletes (23.3 +/- 5.3 y); 10 MMA athletes (24 +/- 2.8 y); 12 BJJ athletes (sex not given in the abstract); 61 judo athletes aged 14 to 17 (37 male); 8 boxers (sex not given in the abstract). None is a direct HR-based criterion validation in grappling or MMA.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Small, mostly observational samples; the MMA external load measure (a torso accelerometer) is not an established criterion for grappling; correlations of sRPE with wellness variables mean the rating reflects state as well as load. Haddad 2017 and Ouergui 2020 point in different directions on whether pre-fatigue affects the rating. No combat-specific sRPE reliability or validity meta-analysis was found.
**Sources:** [F108], [F109], [F110], [F111], [F112], [F038]

### Ask for the session RPE about 30 minutes after training
**Rule:** FightLab convention: prompt for the session RPE roughly 30 minutes after the session ends (the timing used in the original method), and accept a rating given at 10 minutes or later, but not a rating given in the first minutes after the session. The evidence on exact timing is limited and mixed, so the app should not present any one timing as proven.
**Evidence:** In 8 boxers rating easy, moderate and hard standardised sessions (Uchida 2014), session-RPE collected 10 min after exercise was not significantly different from that collected at 30 min at any intensity (hard session: 5.7 +/- 1.0 versus 5.8 +/- 1.9 AU); the authors say 10 min is workable. In 9 prepubescent taekwondo athletes (Lupo 2017), RPE was collected at 1 min and at 30 min; all timing and session combinations correlated with Edwards' HR load, but the large correlation (r = 0.71) appeared only for pre-competitive sessions rated at 30 min, which the authors treat as the best condition. In 15 men doing resistance sessions (Singh 2007), RPE collected at the 5th and 10th minute after exercise differed significantly from the 30th-minute value, while ratings at other times through 30 min did not differ from the 30 min value.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** 8 boxers (3 sessions each, sex not given in the abstract); 9 prepubescent taekwondo athletes (5 girls, 4 boys, 12 y); 15 resistance-trained men (about 27 y). No adult MMA, BJJ or wrestling timing study was found.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Boxers showed no 10 versus 30 min difference, but resistance trainers did differ at 10 min, so the effect may depend on the session type; none of the opened studies tested ratings given hours later (same evening or next day), so no claim can be made on delayed logging. The 30 min convention comes from the original method; evidence for it is mostly no-difference results in small samples.
**Sources:** [F112], [F107], [F113]

### Use the CR10 scale with Foster's wording
**Rule:** Keep the modified CR10 scale (Foster's wording) for session-RPE, since that is the scale used in the validity studies. FightLab convention: do not mix CR10 and 6-20 Borg ratings in the same load calculation. The app should not claim that CR10 is superior to 6-20.
**Evidence:** A mini review (Haddad 2017) counted 36 validity and reliability studies of the session-RPE method, all using the modified CR10 scale proposed by Foster, and reproduces that scale (0 rest to 10 maximal) as the one from Foster 2001. Among the combat-sport studies, Lupo 2017 states use of the CR-10 scale in its abstract and Haddad 2011b in its full text.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Studies across sports and ages, as in Haddad 2017; youth taekwondo (Lupo 2017, Haddad 2011b).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** No direct comparison of CR10 and 6-20 for session load was found; the rule only says the validity evidence is built on CR10. The no-mixing rule is a convention, not a tested finding.
**Sources:** [F038], [F107], [F098]

### Monotony and strain are descriptive indices
**Rule:** FightLab can show monotony (mean daily load divided by its standard deviation over a week) and strain (weekly load times monotony) as descriptive training-pattern indices, and should say that they came from a small illness-monitoring study. They should be described as indicators of how uniform and heavy the week was, not as proven risk scores.
**Evidence:** Foster 1998 reported on 25 experienced athletes who logged session-RPE x duration; illnesses were related to rolling 6-week average load, monotony (daily mean divided by SD) and strain (load x monotony). The author reported that a high percentage of illnesses could be accounted for when individual athletes exceeded individually identifiable training thresholds, mostly related to strain. The full text of Haddad 2017 restates the definitions (monotony as mean over SD of daily load across a week; strain as weekly load x monotony) and says monotony is linked to overtraining when combined with high load.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Foster 1998: 25 experienced athletes (sport and sex not given in the abstract); illness as the outcome.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The abstract reports individual thresholds, not a universal cut-off, and gives no effect sizes. By definition monotony rises sharply when the daily SD is small (for example a very uniform week, or very few sessions), which makes it unstable in small or sparse weeks; this follows arithmetically from the definition and is not from an opened criticism paper. A search for prospective validation papers specific to monotony or strain found none in PubMed beyond the small cohort studies cited in the next rule.
**Sources:** [F040], [F038]

### Monotony and strain illness thresholds are inconsistent
**Rule:** The app can say that monotony and strain have been linked with illness in some athlete cohorts, but that the thresholds differ between studies and none is validated for combat sports. FightLab should not display risk labels or cut-offs derived from them.
**Evidence:** A systematic review of risk factors for acute respiratory illness in athletes (Derman 2022; 48 studies, 19,390 athletes) listed increased training monotony among the associated risk factors (the abstract gives no effect size). In 14 collegiate women soccer players over 9 weeks (Putlur 2004), increases in load, strain and monotony were associated with more illness, and 82% of illnesses were preceded by a drop in salivary IgA. In 12 collegiate women basketball players (Anderson 2003), injuries rose in periods of high training load (r = 0.675). In 32 professional rugby league players over 29 weeks (Thornton 2016), the models flagged strain above 2282 AU, weekly load above 2786 AU and monotony above 0.78 as best predicting self-reported illness.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Derman 2022: athletes aged 15-65, mixed sports, elite and non-elite, no combat-specific breakdown in the abstract. Putlur 2004: 14 NCAA D-III women soccer players. Anderson 2003: 12 NCAA D-III women basketball players. Thornton 2016: 32 male professional rugby league players.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Thresholds vary: Thornton's monotony cut-off (0.78) and strain cut-off are sport and sample specific; the small cohorts rely on associations (not prospective validation) and used illness reports. Anderson 2003 relates injury to load, not to monotony itself. Not applicable to combat sports without separate data.
**Sources:** [F114], [F115], [F116], [F117]

### Combat-sport monotony and strain data are weak and observational
**Rule:** When showing monotony and strain to fighters, treat them as a weekly pattern summary. The app should not tell a fighter that a given monotony or strain value predicts injury, illness or poor performance.
**Evidence:** In 16 elite young wrestlers followed for 46 weeks (Yildiz 2024), the only notable relationships were moderate (r = 0.51 between ACWR and mean weekly load) and strong (r = 0.81 between monotony and strain); the other relationships among load indices, jump performance and Hooper wellness ratings were small and statistically non-significant. In 20 elite junior female boxers over 10 weeks (Aydin 2026), weekly load, monotony and strain fluctuated significantly (p < 0.001) and moved in line with week-to-week neuromuscular performance changes, with inter-individual variability. In 12 BJJ athletes (Campos 2022), the high load, monotony and strain period coincided with greater stress and hormonal and immune changes, with no performance decrement in jump tests.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** 16 elite young wrestlers (46 weeks); 20 elite junior female boxers (mean age 18.9 y, 10 weeks); 12 BJJ athletes (7 weeks).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The strong monotony-strain correlation is largely built in (strain = load x monotony). Findings differ between studies (wrestlers: no link to wellness; boxers: temporal alignment with neuromuscular performance, a descriptive alignment in one group, no prediction test). Results are observational and small, and may not generalise to non-elite adults.
**Sources:** [F118], [F119], [F110]

### Do not present session-RPE indices as injury predictors
**Rule:** FightLab should not present load, ACWR, monotony or strain as injury predictors for fighters. It can say that large increases in load have been associated with injury and illness in studies of mostly team-sport athletes, that the relation is not established in other populations, and that no combat-sport injury-prediction evidence was found.
**Evidence:** A systematic review (Drew 2016; 35 studies, mostly rugby league, cricket and football) found moderate-level evidence of a relationship between training load and injury (most studies, 93%) and illness (75%) but noted a protective effect in some studies (injury 31%, illness 13%), and recommended session-RPE load for team-based sports and avoiding acute spikes. A review of 59 studies in adolescent athletes (Dudley 2023) found only limited or inconsistent evidence for relations between load measures and physical qualities, injury or illness, apart from moderate evidence for throw count and injury and for resistance training volume and strength. A swimming systematic review (Barry 2021; 15 studies) found study quality poor and the relationship between load and injury or illness unclear, with some evidence of a relationship and no evidence for pain.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Mixed team-sport and adolescent athletes; swimmers. No combat sports in the abstracts; none of these reviews was combat-specific.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The Drew review itself reports moderate evidence for a load-injury link, but with limits (one reviewer rating quality, studies added outside the search) and mixed protective findings; Dudley and Barry find the evidence limited or unclear. The aim of this rule is what FightLab can say (no individual injury prediction), not whether load matters.
**Sources:** [F120], [F121], [F122]

### The fitness-fatigue model predicts individual performance poorly
**Rule:** If FightLab shows a fitness-fatigue (CTL/ATL) display, it must not claim to predict a fighter's performance or readiness. It can describe it as a smoothed picture of recent load, because published tests of the model show imprecise and poorly identifiable parameters and weak individual prediction.
**Evidence:** The model can describe past data closely in individual athletes (r = 0.96 across 19 performances in one hammer thrower, Busso 1994; R2 = 0.79 +/- 0.13 in 9 elite swimmers, Hellard 2006). In the swimmers, however, the 95% bootstrap confidence intervals of the key parameters were wide (for example 38 with CI 17 to 59, and 19 with CI 6 to 32, for two time constants), and some parameters were so strongly correlated that they could not be interpreted separately, even though refitting with one performance removed gave stable estimates (Hellard 2006). In 11 swimmers followed for 61 weeks (Busso 2023), Banister's model and a variable dose-response model ranked best, but their errors when predicting later performance were 2.02% and 2.69% (mean absolute percentage error, Banister model) and the authors judged the predictions not good enough for planning an individual's training. A Bayesian analysis of elite short-track speed skaters in two independent datasets (Marchal 2025) found the model ill-conditioned, with fitness and fatigue parameters that could not be pinned down, and overfitted: adding the fatigue terms did not improve prediction (p > 0.40), which questions what the fatigue component means biologically. A review of systems models (Taha 2003) concluded that they are empirical descriptions whose predictions have disagreed with measured adaptations and performance in published data, and whose link to physiology is unclear.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** 1 hammer thrower (Busso 1994); 9 elite swimmers (Hellard 2006); 11 swimmers over two seasons (Busso 2023); 7 and 14 national elite short-track speed skaters (Marchal 2025); all individual sports with frequent performance tests. No combat sports.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Busso 2023 still found that the best models gave relevant approximations of the training-performance relationship. Exponentially weighted impulse-response models fitted three elite swimmers better than rolling averages (SEE 0.57 to 0.73 s versus 1.32 to 1.36 s; Mitchell 2020), but that is goodness of fit in 3 swimmers, not prediction, and neural network models fitted better still. Findings are concordant across studies but all come from swimming, throwing and skating with regular performance testing, not combat sports. The original Banister 1975 paper was not opened.
**Sources:** [F041], [F123], [F042], [F124], [F125], [F126]

### The 42-day and 7-day PMC constants are conventions
**Rule:** FightLab convention: if a PMC is built, state that the 42-day (CTL) and 7-day (ATL) time constants and the TSB difference are conventions chosen for display, not values fitted to or validated for the individual. The app should say that individual time constants can differ widely and that different load measures give different model results. It must not attach TSB thresholds (form or fresh zones) and must not output injury or performance predictions from these values.
**Evidence:** When the model was fitted to individual elite swimmers, the estimated time constants had very wide confidence intervals (for example 38 with CI 17 to 59, and 19 with CI 6 to 32; units not given in the opened text; Hellard 2006), so no single constant fits everyone. In 9 people doing 8 weeks of interval cycling (Vermeire 2021), the model error was similar whether load was Banister TRIMP, Lucia TRIMP, Edwards TRIMP, a training stress score, kilojoules or session-RPE (about 558 for session-RPE and 596 to 643 for the others), but the load measures changed differently over time and the day of greatest positive training influence differed by up to 19.6 days between them, so they are not interchangeable. In 3 world top-8 swimmers (Mitchell 2020), exponentially weighted models fitted performance better than plain rolling averages. No opened source defines or validates the 42-day and 7-day constants or any TSB thresholds.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** 9 elite swimmers (Hellard 2006); 9 participants in a short interval-cycling study (Vermeire 2021, training status not given in the abstract); 3 world-ranked swimmers (Mitchell 2020). No combat sports.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Very small samples. Vermeire found that session-RPE fitted as well as HR-based methods in a short cycling study, which supports sRPE as an input but not the 42 and 7 day constants. The 42 and 7 day values and TSB thresholds were searched for and not found in any opened scientific source.
**Sources:** [F041], [F127], [F126]

### A PMC display is a labelled FightLab convention
**Rule:** If the PMC is built, label it "FightLab convention: smoothed load trend (42-day and 7-day weighted averages; difference shown as a load balance)" with an emerging-evidence note. It must not show TSB, form or readiness thresholds, must not output injury or performance predictions, and should not suggest taper timing. The input (session-RPE load) has moderate support as a load measure in athletes in general and emerging support in combat sports.
**Evidence:** Synthesis of the session-RPE and fitness-fatigue rules above: session-RPE has moderate support as a load measure in general (mostly team sports) and only small-sample, emerging support in taekwondo and karate, thinner still in grappling and MMA; the fitness-fatigue model's imprecise, poorly identifiable parameters and weak individual predictions have been documented in swimmers, a hammer thrower and short-track speed skaters (Hellard 2006, Busso 2023, Marchal 2025); load-injury evidence is mixed outside team sports; and no study of the model, or of CTL, ATL or TSB thresholds, in combat sports was found.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Derived from the sources above; no combat-sport PMC study exists in what was opened.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Coaching use of CTL, ATL and TSB is widespread, but that practice outruns the opened evidence. The frequent, repeatable, fight-specific performance tests the model needs are rarely available to fighters. A fixed-constant exponentially weighted display may still serve as a descriptive load summary, as the swimmer fit comparison (Mitchell 2020) hints, without any predictive claim.
**Sources:** [F041], [F123], [F042], [F127], [F126], [F035], [F108], [F120]

## Energy expenditure and PAL

### Use 2024 Compendium METs as combat intensity anchors
**Rule:** When FightLab needs a MET anchor for a combat session, use the 2024 Adult Compendium and name the entry used. Boxing: in ring, general 12.3; punching bag, general 5.8 (paced at 60, 120 and 180 punches per minute: 7.0, 8.5, 10.8); sparring 7.8; simulated boxing round 9.3. Martial arts at moderate pace (the entry lists judo, jujitsu, karate, kickboxing, taekwondo, Muay Thai) 10.3; novice slower-pace practice 5.3; judo 11.3; taekwondo combat simulation 14.3; kickboxing 7.3; competitive wrestling (one 5-min match) 6.0, which the Compendium marks as an estimated value. Sparring, moderate-pace martial arts and the paced bag entries classify as vigorous (>= 6.0 METs); novice slow-pace practice and the general punching-bag entry classify as moderate (3.0-5.9). If the app still holds 2011 values, update them (in ring 12.8 to 12.3, punching bag 5.5 to 5.8). There is no entry for MMA, Brazilian jiu-jitsu or grappling: use the generic martial-arts or wrestling entry and flag the approximation.
**Evidence:** The 2024 Adult Compendium lists 1114 activities across 22 major headings (912 with measured and 202 with estimated METs) and defines moderate as 3.0-5.9 and vigorous as 6.0 METs or more. Its website table, full-list PDF and edition tracking guide give the combat values above. The tracking guide shows that in-ring boxing (12.8 in 2011) and the general punching-bag entry (5.5 in 2011) were revised in 2024, that sparring (7.8), moderate-pace martial arts (10.3) and novice practice (5.3) are unchanged since 2011, that wrestling has been 6.0 since 1993, and that the paced bag entries, simulated round, taekwondo combat simulation, judo and kickboxing are new in 2024. The website marks estimated codes in red; among the combat entries only wrestling is marked. The authors say live sports such as boxing remain hard to measure and name wrestling as an example of an estimated MET. No entry for mixed martial arts, jiu-jitsu as a separate sport, or grappling exists in the table or PDF.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** Healthy adults aged 19-59 (the 2024 update removed data from people aged 60 and over); not athlete-specific; one value per activity with no adjustment for sex, body size or skill. The underlying study for each non-estimated combat entry was not opened.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Some entries do not fit measured athlete data: kickboxing 7.3 sits well below the generic moderate-pace martial-arts value 10.3 and below the 9.39 METs measured in a simulated Muay Thai match (see "Hard combat sessions cost about 8-13 kcal per minute"); in-ring boxing (12.3) is far above sparring (7.8). The wrestling value is estimated, not measured, and the Compendium says live boxing is poorly measured. Treat the table as a classification scale, not as the measured cost of a given gym session. The Compendium's own documents code kickboxing inconsistently (15450 in the tracking guide, 15457 on the website and in the PDF).
**Sources:** [F043], [F128], [F129], [F130]

### METs classify intensity but do not give individual kcal
**Rule:** Show session kcal as an approximate range and label it as an estimate. Do not present MET x kg x hours as an individual's true expenditure, and do not use it to make fine calorie adjustments. FightLab convention: show a +/- band around the estimate instead of a single exact number.
**Evidence:** The Compendium authors state that it can be a starting point for prescribing individual activities but does not reflect precise individual energy expenditure. A cited example found about 8-15% higher energy cost in women with obesity than in women without at the same treadmill speeds and grades. 1 MET is defined as 3.5 ml/kg/min (about 1 kcal/kg/h), a fixed standard, not each person's measured resting rate. The website explains that when true resting metabolic rate is below 3.5 ml/kg/min the standard MET understates the person's MET, and cites Kozey et al. as finding that standard METs understate measured METs 89% of the time and misclassify the intensity category 12.2% of the time. A corrected MET (standard MET divided by a Harris-Benedict-predicted RMR) is offered for individual use, but the site says corrected values should not replace standard METs for population classification and that it has not been shown that corrected values are superior; critics (Howley) say the ratio should not be called a MET.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** The source is the Compendium authors' own caveats (the position of the reference itself); the quantitative examples come from non-athlete adults. No combat-sport data on the size of the individual error.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Whether corrected METs are better is disputed (Howley versus Kozey et al.). The direction of error for muscular athletes with a high resting rate per kg is not tested in the opened sources.
**Sources:** [F043], [F131]

### Hard combat sessions cost about 8-13 kcal per minute
**Rule:** For hard striking or sparring work, expect roughly 8-13 kcal/min in trained adults; for technique-focused or light-contact sessions expect clearly less. Use a session-content factor (bag, pad or sparring rounds versus drills) in addition to modality. Do not add a large afterburn on top. FightLab convention: keep a lower factor for drill and technique blocks than for pad, bag and sparring blocks.
**Evidence:** (a) Eight healthy men used to non-contact boxing training did a 60-min lab session with indirect calorimetry: 2821 +/- 190 kJ (range 2519-3079 kJ), about 674 kcal or 11.2 kcal/min (converted at 4.184 kJ per kcal); the authors equate it to running about 9 km in an hour. (b) Ten male athletes in a simulated Muay Thai match with a portable gas analyser: 10.75 +/- 1.58 (SEM) kcal/min, equal to 9.39 METs. (c) Twenty Indian national female boxers during sparring: average 12.7 +/- 1.3 and maximum 14.4 +/- 1.6 kcal/min, estimated from each boxer's own heart-rate to oxygen-uptake regression, not measured directly. (d) Fifteen men and 14 women (mean age 25.6 years, 71.8 kg, not described as athletes) doing 10 x 3-min heavy-bag bouts with indirect calorimetry: 8.0 kcal/min, 241 kcal in 30 min, heart rate 156, RPE 13.8. (e) A 70-min karate practice (participants' sex not stated in the abstract) cost 2355.4 +/- 316.3 kJ, about 563 kcal or 8.0 kcal/min; in women karate practitioners the same type of practice cost 1120 +/- 64 kJ, about 268 kcal or 3.8 kcal/min, rated light to moderate. (f) Five-minute post-exercise oxygen consumption after 70 min of karate was small (28 to 39 kJ) and not different from rest.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Small samples: n=8 men (non-contact boxing), n=10 men (simulated Muay Thai match), n=20 national-level women (boxing sparring, HR-based), n=29 young non-athlete adults (heavy bag), karate samples of unstated size (one study of women, one with sex not stated). Mostly young adults. No measured MMA, BJJ, wrestling or judo training-session kcal found.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The wide spread across studies (3.8 to 14.4 kcal/min) reflects session content (drills versus sparring), body mass (absolute kcal/min, body mass mostly not given) and method (indirect calorimetry versus HR regression). The highest values (female boxers) come from an HR-based method, which can overstate cost (see the next rule). Using the Compendium sparring value 7.8 METs and 1 MET about 1 kcal/kg/h gives about 0.13 kcal/kg/min, about 9.8 kcal/min for a 75 kg adult, inside the measured range. Studies are from 1997-2014 and none measured a full real gym session with rest periods and instruction.
**Sources:** [F132], [F093], [F133], [F134], [F135], [F136]

### Heart rate can overstate the cost of technique training
**Rule:** Do not infer kcal from heart rate alone for technique, kata or pad-drill blocks. If a session is logged with heart rate, say that the resulting kcal may overstate the true cost of drill-type work.
**Evidence:** In women karate practitioners, during sparring-technique exercises, kata and the whole 70-min practice, mean percent of maximal heart rate and of heart-rate reserve were well above accepted aerobic-training thresholds while percent of oxygen-uptake reserve was below its threshold; the authors rated the training light to moderate. In an earlier study of the same kind of session by the same group (sex not stated in the abstract), mean percent of maximal heart rate was slightly above its threshold and mean percent of VO2max slightly below for kata and for the whole practice. Heart rate therefore pointed to a higher intensity than oxygen uptake in this type of training.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Karate practitioners; sample sizes not stated in the abstracts; two studies from one research group (1999, 2002).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Only karate; untested in boxing, MMA, wrestling or grappling. The studies compare intensity classifications, not kcal estimates, so the size of any kcal overstatement is unknown.
**Sources:** [F135], [F136]

### Wearable kcal are rough and unvalidated in combat sports
**Rule:** Label watch- or strap-derived kcal as a rough estimate, never as a precise value, and do not use it to set daily intake targets. Say plainly that no wearable has been validated for kcal in boxing, MMA, Muay Thai, kickboxing, wrestling, judo or BJJ training, and that error may be larger in intermittent, high-intensity and grappling work than in steady exercise.
**Evidence:** A systematic review of 158 studies covering nine commercial brands found that no brand measured energy expenditure accurately, while steps and heart rate were more accurate in the lab. A random-effects meta-analysis of 60 studies (104 effect sizes) of wrist- and arm-worn devices against indirect calorimetry, room calorimeters or doubly labelled water found that accuracy depended on activity type, with large heterogeneity (I2 above 75% for many devices); adding heart rate or heat sensing to accelerometry reduced error in most activity types. A living umbrella review of 24 systematic reviews (249 validation studies) found that wearables mostly underestimated energy expenditure, with a mean bias of about -3 kcal/min (-3%) and errors ranging from -21.27% to +14.76%; mean absolute error for activity-intensity measures was 29-80%. In 13 elite female soccer players on an intermittent high-intensity course, three GPS and accelerometer trackers significantly underestimated total exercise energy (absolute ICC 0.24-0.39), more so as expenditure rose; excluding EPOC reduced the bias but did not remove it. No validation against a criterion method in real combat-sport training was found.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** General adults across many device studies; the soccer study is 13 elite women. No study in boxers, MMA athletes, judoka, wrestlers or BJJ athletes exists in the literature searched.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Direction and size of error differ by device and activity: the umbrella review shows a slight average underestimate, while the karate data suggest HR-based intensity can be overstated. The only combat-flavoured validation found (a Wii boxing exergame in 21 students) used another accelerometer as the reference, so it says nothing about true expenditure. That error is larger in combat sessions is an inference, not a measured result.
**Sources:** [F137], [F138], [F044], [F139], [F140]

### FAO/WHO/UNU PAL bands are the reference scale
**Rule:** Use the FAO/WHO/UNU bands as the reference scale for habitual activity: sedentary or light 1.40-1.69, active or moderately active 1.70-1.99, vigorous 2.00-2.40, and tell users that a PAL above 2.40 is hard to maintain over a long period in general populations. PAL here means total daily energy expenditure as a multiple of basal metabolic rate.
**Evidence:** Table 5.3 of the FAO/WHO/UNU 2004 report (consultation held in 2001) lists these three bands and notes that values above 2.40 are difficult to maintain over a long period. The report states that PAL values sustained by free-living adult populations range from about 1.40 to 2.40. It describes the vigorous category as people who engage in strenuous work or strenuous leisure for several hours; its examples are women who swim or dance an average of two hours a day and manual labourers working for several hours daily. It gives one hour a day of moderate-to-vigorous exercise (jogging, cycling, aerobic dancing, sports) as raising a person's PAL from 1.55 to 1.75, that is about +0.2 per hour. It also gives about +/- 8-10% variation in requirement around each band's midpoint and says some individuals lie outside the band limits.
**Grade:** strong
**Applies to:** General free-living adult populations worldwide (including occupational workloads); not an athlete-specific document. Athletes are not described as a category.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Athletes training in structured daily blocks can exceed 2.4 (see the next two rules). The +0.2 PAL per hour example is for one example scenario and is not a combat-specific constant.
**Sources:** [F045]

### Athletes can exceed PAL 2.0, with a ceiling near 2.4-2.5
**Rule:** Do not hard-cap PAL at 2.0 for athletes who train hard on most days. What the evidence supports: 2.00-2.40 is the FAO/WHO/UNU vigorous band; doubly labelled water reviews find that vigorously active people and young adults training with unrestricted food reach about 2.0-2.4; the upper limit for sustained PAL is about 2.2-2.5 in general populations, with an energy-supply limit of about 2.5x BMR beyond which body stores are drawn down; sustained values above this are reported mainly in professional and endurance athletes who eat extra energy-dense food. Tell users that values above about 2.4-2.5 are hard to sustain and need a measured basis. FightLab convention (product choice, not evidence): the default PAL, the highest value the app allows, and the trigger for raising it (for example two hard sessions on most days) are app settings to be reviewed against the athlete's body-weight trend.
**Evidence:** Westerterp's 2018 review of doubly labelled water studies reports a maximum PAL (TDEE/REE) of 2.00-2.40 in people with a vigorously active lifestyle, the same range reached by young adults in exercise training when intake is unrestricted, and higher values in professional athletes who eat additional energy-dense food. His 2001 review puts the upper limit in the general population at 2.2-2.5 and in endurance athletes, fed carbohydrate during exercise, at 4.0-5.0, while trained climbers on Everest reached only 2.0-2.7. A 2004 review reports that an upper limit of 2.5 from cross-sectional data is confirmed by training interventions. A compilation of extreme endurance events found that sustained expenditure falls with event duration and plateaus below 3x BMR for events of 0.5 to over 250 days, and places an alimentary energy-supply limit at about 2.5x BMR. A 2025 narrative review of 11 DLW studies in field sports found PAL 2.2 +/- 0.4 in rugby and 1.7 +/- 0.2 in soccer. The FAO/WHO/UNU report gives 2.00-2.40 as the vigorous band and notes that PAL above 2.40 is hard to maintain.
**Grade:** moderate
**Applies to:** DLW studies in general populations and in endurance and field-sport athletes, mostly men. Endurance-athlete values (4.0-5.0) do not transfer to combat sports. Combat athletes are represented only by one wrestling study (see the next rule).
**Individual variation / conflicts:** Reviews divide TDEE by REE or by BMR, which differ slightly, so the numbers are not strictly interchangeable. The ceiling comes mainly from non-combat and long-duration data. Short heavy blocks can exceed 2.5 without being sustainable (FAO cites 4.5-4.7 during three weeks of competitive cycling). Any specific product default is a convention.
**Sources:** [F046], [F047], [F141], [F048], [F142], [F045]

### Collegiate wrestlers measured a PAL near 2.6
**Rule:** Tell combat athletes in heavy training that the only doubly labelled water study of combat athletes found (10 collegiate wrestlers training about 3 hours a day) a measured PAL of about 2.6, higher than equations that assume 2.0 or 2.2, and that their food diaries came out 19% below measured expenditure while body weight was held stable. FightLab convention (not evidence): for heavy training blocks the app may offer planning values of 2.2-2.6 and asks the athlete to check body weight and performance over 2-3 weeks; this range extrapolates one small study plus the PAL bands and is not a validated prescription.
**Evidence:** Ten male Japanese collegiate wrestlers (age 20.4 +/- 0.5 years; 4 light and 6 middle weight class; body mass 73.1 +/- 8.1 kg) were measured with doubly labelled water for about a week in the winter training season. They trained 6 +/- 1 days a week for 172 +/- 15 min a day. Total energy expenditure was 17.9 +/- 2.5 MJ/day (4283 +/- 590 kcal/day) against self-reported intake of 14.4 +/- 3.3 MJ/day (3446 +/- 799 kcal/day), a 19% gap, while participants were asked to keep body weight stable. Measured PAL (TEE divided by measured REE, which the paper treats as close to BMR) was 2.58 +/- 0.34 overall (light 2.47 +/- 0.42, middle 2.73 +/- 0.06). Two requirement equations using PAL 2.2 and 2.0 underestimated needs.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Ten male collegiate wrestlers in Japan, one season, about one week of DLW. No women, no MMA, judo, boxing, Muay Thai or BJJ athletes, no professionals, no comparison of once-daily versus twice-daily training.
**Individual variation / conflicts:** A single small study at a high training load (172 min/day) that may not represent amateur fighters. Self-reported intake was 19% below measured expenditure, so intake logs are not a reliable check of requirements. PAL also depends on activity outside training, which was not reported.
**Sources:** [F143]

### Inform and refer on low energy availability
**Rule:** Explain energy availability (intake minus exercise energy cost, per kg of fat-free mass) as background, describing about 45 kcal/kg FFM/day as a research reference for a healthy level, not as a precise target the app can compute, because field estimates of each part are unreliable. Tell combat athletes that limited data report a high share at risk of low energy availability, at recreational as well as elite level, and that it rises during pre-competition weight loss. Do not lower an intake target because of a rough exercise-kcal estimate. If an athlete reports very low intake or possible symptoms, give information and a prompt to see a sports physician or dietitian; never diagnose.
**Evidence:** A 2018 review defines energy availability as energy intake minus the energy cost of exercise, relative to fat-free mass, links low availability to the REDs syndrome in female and male athletes with dose-response effects below a healthy level of about 45 kcal/kg FFM/day, and warns that the lack of a universal protocol, the resources needed and residual errors in each component make field estimates unreliable and leave the low threshold unclear. The IOC 2023 REDs consensus (abstract) defines low energy availability as inadequate intake relative to exercise expenditure and introduces a clinical assessment tool for detection and diagnosis by clinicians. In 84 female combat athletes (judo, freestyle wrestling, sanda) in Beijing, 45.2% (n=38) were at increased risk of low energy availability by questionnaire and 21.4% (n=18) had high eating-disorder risk, with no difference between elite and recreational athletes; of 11 judo athletes followed before a competition, 6 were in low energy availability four weeks out and all 11 by competition week, during a weight-loss phase. In 117 elite Korean male taekwondo players (59 high school, 58 college) with five days of accelerometer and dietary records, the nutritional adequacy ratio for total energy was 0.82, below recommended.
**Grade:** emerging
**Applies to:** Review and consensus sources on athletes generally; combat-specific data from 84 Chinese female combat athletes (11 followed longitudinally) and 117 Korean male taekwondo players, half of them high-school adolescents. Pre-competition data overlap with weight cutting, covered under "Making weight and rehydration".
**Individual variation / conflicts:** The 45 kcal/kg FFM/day level and any low threshold are debated and sensitive to measurement error. The IOC 2023 statement was opened by abstract only, so no thresholds from it are used. Questionnaire risk is not a diagnosis. Dietary records under-report intake (see the wrestler PAL rule), so low recorded intake may partly reflect reporting error.
**Sources:** [F049], [F020], [F050], [F144]
